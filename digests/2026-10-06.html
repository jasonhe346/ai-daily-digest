Frontier labs & models

Reflection launches Beam, a 501B open-weight model. Nvidia-backed Reflection AI introduced Beam, which Axios had previewed over the weekend as the company’s first open-weight model, positioned to target top Chinese open systems like DeepSeek and Qwen and to give Western enterprises a “bank-approved” base on which to build proprietary, low-cost AI (corroborated by AI Weekly Espresso, TLDR AI, and The Neuron). Beam is a 501-billion-parameter sparse mixture-of-experts model with 23 billion active parameters, trained for coding, reasoning, and agentic workloads. It used 23.8 trillion pretraining tokens and more than 100 million reinforcement-learning rollouts, trained on 10,500 Nvidia GB300 GPUs over roughly four weeks. Reflection says Beam needs three to four times less computing power than comparable open models. Open weights, a technical report, and developer artifacts are planned for release later in October. (Reflection blog)

DeepSeek narrows the US-China model gap to ~3%. DeepSeek’s V4.1 Flash, released September 10, scored 81.1 on LiveBench against Anthropic’s top score of 83.4 — the smallest US-China top-model gap Bloomberg Intelligence has tracked, per analyst Robert Lea. The roughly 3% spread is down from about 9% in May and 15% earlier in 2026. Separately, Bloomberg reports DeepSeek is finalizing a pre-IPO round of at least $12 billion (80 billion yuan), with Tencent and battery giant CATL among the biggest backers, ahead of a Hong Kong IPO targeted for Q1 2027; the final tally could approach 100 billion yuan.

Liquid says it costs 19x to 200x less than GPT-6.1 Sol and Claude Opus 5.5 and answers significantly faster on every task. The model outputs probabilities for yes/no, choice, or score questions in a single forward pass without generating tokens, making text decisions in 200–300 milliseconds.

Company & product developments

OpenAI adds invisible text watermarks in the EU. On Monday OpenAI said it will embed an invisible signal in text that ChatGPT and Codex generate for users in the EU, to satisfy the AI Act’s requirement that machine-written text be identifiable by software. The method, called textGrain, lives in the model’s word-choice patterns — invisible to readers but findable by a special detector — and OpenAI plans to publish it for others to build on. Rollout details: API developers can opt in to watermarked text worldwide starting now for select models (off by default); ChatGPT and Codex users in the EU get it on every plan over the coming weeks, with no global consumer rollout at launch; the detector stays private, with approved researchers and expert organizations able to apply for access while the public cannot. OpenAI says the watermark left its newest model’s benchmark scores essentially unchanged and that it matched or beat other methods, including Google’s SynthID for text. Crucially, OpenAI itself documented where the watermark breaks: short passages are detected ~80% of the time versus ~95% for passages twice as long (at a 1% false-alarm rate); math is harder still because there are fewer ways to word an answer; and swapping 10% of words for synonyms dropped detection from ~92% to 66%, while swapping 25% dropped it to 17%. OpenAI stresses a watermark cannot reveal who wrote a passage, how much a human edited it, or whether it is accurate — and that a missing watermark proves nothing about human authorship. The Neuron’s take: a light rewrite “mostly erases the signal,” making this more a compliance move for Brussels than a shield against copy-and-paste; an open question is who checks a document against every lab’s separate private detector. (OpenAI post)

Policy & safety

Nathan Lambert: the cyber-risk discourse is broken. In Interconnects, Lambert argues the open-weights cyber-risk debate is heading toward lose-lose policy that would both limit US competitiveness and increase long-term cyber risk. He maps three camps: frontier-lab leaders and the national-security community who see open weights as an untenable societal risk; Western moderates (including himself, Hugging Face after the OpenAI incident, and Joshua Saxe) who argue open weights are necessary for defense and banning them makes the world less safe; and Chinese companies continuing to release capable open models under their own risk assessments. His critique of Anthropic’s recent report on GLM-5.3 as an offensive cyber tool is not the technical work but its failure to engage cross-cutting questions — what happens if open models are banned, and why Chinese firms deem these models safe to release. He notes that public evidence to date shows closed models (e.g., OpenAI’s) behind most documented AI-enabled cyberattacks, suggesting “Open Dangerous, Closed Safe” may really be “Open Unsafe, Closed Unsafe.” His key logical claim: if you’d ban the latest open-weight models to slow cyber-risk diffusion, you’d also need to make public APIs for frontier closed models illegal, since closed capabilities likely outpace guardrails and banning only open models would widen the offense-defense gap — especially for air-gapped government networks where open weights are the only deployable option. On China, he argues Chinese labs do care about safety but arrive at it endogenously (techno-optimism, government focus on political stability, mandatory registration of major model releases including evaluations originally built for information control), with earlier political oversight than the US but less compute spent on safety testing (he notes comprehensive evals on a frontier model like “Kimi K3” could cost tens of millions). He frames the right debate as “what is the minimum compute a lab should spend on safety testing before each release,” says he wouldn’t require Chinese labs to match Anthropic/OpenAI, and argues the labs’ institutions don’t clearly prioritize safety over business value (citing the HuggingFace–OpenAI incident and the Hacktron hacking of OpenAI). Finally, he contends the “Claude Mythos as new cyber weapon” framing proved wrong — GLM-5.3 crossed that capability threshold over a month ago with little public evidence of catastrophe — making open-weight fear-mongers’ falsifiable prediction (that current open models will cripple cyber infrastructure) look set to fail. (Interconnects)

Strata runs a 125B MoE on a 12GB consumer GPU. The open-source engine Strata claims to run Qwen3.8-Flash-Next — a 125-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts — on a single 12GB consumer GPU with 32GB of system RAM, by offloading experts across host memory. Throughput varies by card; the project pegs an RTX 3090 at roughly 100–140 tokens per second.

RemoveMacAI, a new CLI tool from developer Om Lahore, reversibly disables Apple Intelligence on macOS 27 Golden Gate and reclaims 12–30GB of disk space. Framefields: rendering for coding agents. An npm package built for coding agents, Framefields enables agents to create Photoshop-inspired compositing/VFX, After Effects-style motion/typography/keyframing, and Blender-style 3D scenes, cameras, and models. The agent writes a TypeScript composition, checks frames, and renders an MP4; it renders directly on GPU hardware in Node.js and modern WebGPU browsers. (GitHub)

GRID spreadsheet tools for agents. GRID gives AI agents dedicated tools to read, edit, and generate spreadsheets — pitched as faster, more robust, and using fewer tokens.

Building an AI-native software factory. A 19-minute practical guide, using Uber’s software factory as the case study, covers the infrastructure needed around coding agents at scale: cloud execution, model gateways, agent identity, testing, migrations, code review, and economics like cost per merged pull request. (theaithinker.com)

Building a marketing team in Codex. AI Adopters Club walks through setting up a small AI team in OpenAI’s Codex: a main agent that coordinates and can spawn subagents (separate work threads with their own context), with specialist profiles (a “planner” that develops the brief and a “content producer” that writes from it) — recommending starting with just two to limit handoffs. It explains Skills (reusable job instructions, e.g., turning an approved brief into three posts and landing-page copy), Tools (actions like reading files or web search), MCP (connecting Codex to additional tools/context), and Plugins (bundling skills and MCP connections), noting that installing a skill doesn’t grant account access.

Field & industry commentary

AI skill of the day: make your chatbot argue back. Lawyer Dr. Niklas Schmidt explains sycophancy — chatbots are tuned on human ratings, and people rate agreement higher than correction, so asking for “a balanced view” only changes tone. His structural fix: (1) draft in one message, critique in a separate one; (2) give the critic a side and incentive to win; (3) test the pushback and demand harder counterarguments if it’s soft; (4) keep your hoped-for answer out of the question. He provides a prompt instructing the AI to act as a rival with incentive to defeat your position, listing the three strongest counterarguments, the question you’d least want to answer, any collapse-inducing false assumption, which points it would concede, and marking unverified facts. (llms-for-lawyers.com)

The future of mathematics. Terry Tao argues math remains a core capacity for human reasoning even as the field changes; future historians may see this as the start of a new era, with communal norms, values, and expectations breaking down and needing replacements. (Tao’s blog)
