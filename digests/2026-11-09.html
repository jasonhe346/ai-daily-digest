Research & capability developments

METR report: AI is already dramatically accelerating AI R&D at Anthropic (RSI approaching). METR published an extensive deep-access report (covered at length by Zvi Mowshowitz and TLDR AI via planned-obsolescence.org) measuring how much Anthropic’s own AI is accelerating its AI research, and thus how close we are to recursive self-improvement. The headline finding: as of October 2026, Anthropic is improving model capabilities on well-specified tasks at roughly twice the rate it did between March 2024 and October 2025 (95% CI ~1.3x–3x), and a majority (perhaps ~80%) of that acceleration is attributable to Anthropic’s extensive use of AI to do R&D. This is several months ahead of the AI 2027 timeline; a naive extrapolation says full RSI could be less than a year away, with no observed bottlenecks blocking further acceleration in the next 12 months.

  • Methodology and access: Anthropic offered “unprecedented” access — internal benchmark scores and AECI (Anthropic’s fork of the Epoch Capabilities Index) for unreleased models, pretraining/RL/compute metrics across domains, headcount and attribution data, lines of code and pull requests, AI agent inference spend over time, internal documents on autonomous research contributions, self-reported uplift surveys, and ~15 interviews with key staff. Models referenced: an “Unreleased 2022” model, Model 2 (trained July 2026), Model 3 (Aug–Sep 2026), Model 4 (Sep–Oct 2026), plus a redacted forecast of a future model.
  • Key empirical findings: Since October 2025, AECI benchmark scores grew at ~1.9x the pre-October-2025 rate (95% CI 1.5–2.8x) — roughly two years of historical progress in one year. METR is “confident the acceleration was not due to an acceleration in the scaling of training compute.” Interviews/surveys are consistent with AI causing a doubling in the pace of progress; staff self-report dramatic uplift (recent Slack polls reaching 12x in October 2026). Importantly, because overall progress only doubled while compute is a constraint (Amdahl’s law), the labor uplift to researchers must be far higher — Ajeya Cotra estimates a ~4–9x speedup in labor is needed to double overall progress, and Ryan Greenblatt’s public modeling yields ~4.5x acceleration of AI software R&D specifically (and the whole company’s R&D going ~4–5x faster).
  • Redactions: Anthropic redacted “a majority of the evidence METR found most informative,” including detailed acceleration/attribution breakdowns, the compute/data/algorithmic decomposition, forecasts of future-model metrics, and bottleneck analysis — described as “aimed at removing confidential information that could accelerate other AI developers.” This drew heavy criticism: METR notes the public must “rely on our judgment more than ideal”; Greenblatt says conclusions beyond the raw 2x benchmark figure “depend significantly on trust”; Seán Ó hÉigeartaigh called it frustrating to advise policymakers without being able to point to evidence. A study of Anthropic’s compute allocation was postponed (Daniel Kokotajlo: “what the fuck?… the foundation on which pacing verification depends”). Nathan Calvin noted China could likely already access the redacted info given Anthropic’s “swiss cheese” security for everything but model weights, making the redactions mostly a barrier to good-faith observers.
  • Researcher quotes: Anonymized interviewees ranked from enthusiastic to skeptical. The “yes” voters cited “80% reduction in time between idea and experiment” and “>2 years of progress in 1 year, 4 isn’t crazy,” with inflection points at Opus 4.5/4.6 and a big jump at “Mythos-preview.” Skeptics attributed gains to hiring or redacted non-AI factors (one guessed only 15–20% is from AI). The dominant model: AIs now run experiments cheaply, making the bottleneck idea-generation and selection.
  • Reactions and caveats: Toby Ord argued that allowing a “level shift” from the Mythos pre-train drops the slope change to ~1.4x; Buck Shlegeris countered that the (unredacted) graph is not the main basis for the conclusion. Including non-Anthropic models lowers the estimate to 1.4x. The August 2026 Risk Report’s “less than a factor of 2” claim turned out to mean 1.97 (“approximately 2”). Many researchers reported being shaken (Jake Mendel now >50% on superintelligence within 18 months; Dane Sherburn and Trevor Levin “feel sick”). Skeptics like Dylan Bowman (Apollo) and Peter Wildeford attribute much of the public-benchmark gain to Anthropic catching up on RL from a low baseline. Zvi, Kelsey Piper and Congressmen Alex Bores and Greg Casar call for Congress to subpoena/read the unredacted report and consider banning RSI — noting the difficulty of legally defining RSI. Anthropic cofounder Sam McCandlish defended the redactions and promised Anthropic’s own analysis “in the coming weeks,” including its RSP standing.

PSI says an unreleased Anthropic model proved the Aaronson-Ambainis conjecture. Startup Physical Superintelligence (PSI) claims its research system — running copies of an unreleased Anthropic model — produced a full proof of the Aaronson-Ambainis conjecture, a key open problem in quantum complexity theory stuck since 2008, by “day three” of a run (and then improved the result twice). The conjecture concerns whether quantum computers are truly exponentially faster than classical ones on arbitrary (structureless) problems; informally, if a Boolean function’s value genuinely depends on its inputs, then at least one input must be highly influential, letting a classical algorithm efficiently hunt the influential variables and keep pace with the quantum algorithm (extra work grows polynomially, not exponentially). The practical upshot: quantum computers are not secretly faster at everything — but, per The Quantum Insider, the result counts queries not total compute time, holds for most inputs not all, “a bit more work” can still be huge, and it does not show classical computers can mimic all quantum computing (notably not Shor’s algorithm, which exploits structure). The provenance: human David Turturean set up the system, picked the problem, ran it and prompted the write-up. To establish trust amid recent AI-math-credit disputes (e.g., mathematicians including Terence Tao pushing back on OpenAI’s 300+ claimed results), PSI formalized the proof in Lean (a strict proof-checker) and also wrote the conjecture statement itself in Lean, publishing the code. Four Stanford researchers who solved a smaller version in August read the proof and called it a major result, though they’re thanked in the paper (feedback from inside the circle); no comment yet from namesakes Scott Aaronson or Andris Ambainis. (Aaronson’s own blog and TLDR covered it; TLDR framed it as part of a wave of AI-produced proofs validated via Lean certificates. On Friday, Axiom released a giant Lean formalization of the 7,500-page Stacks Project built mostly by AI agents.)

Co-Design Bench exposes the “mostly done” gap in coding agents. A new benchmark (arxiv 2610.35138) tested coding agents on 273 real app-editing tasks against 2,331 hidden expert-written acceptance criteria the models never saw. The best of ten models satisfied 69.9% of criteria on an average task but fully completed only 26.4% of tasks (a task counts only if every criterion passes). The grading method — a judge checks each criterion against evidence, then a separate verifier challenges every pass — is the reusable lesson: make the AI generate an explicit checklist up front, then use a fresh “skeptic” chat whose only job is to try to break the finished work, marking each item PASS/FAIL/UNCERTAIN.

Anthropic published work on how AI agents can build long-term memory by capturing experiences during interactions and reorganizing them during idle periods through a process it calls “dreaming,” examining how different memory components contribute to retention. Separately, in “Project EVE,” Anthropic used Claude to test complex decision-making by having it participate in the MMORPGs EVE Online and EVE Frontier.

Company & product developments

Anthropic buys ~$3.4B of gas generators (1.7 GW). Per the FT (reported Nov 8, via CNBC), Anthropic committed roughly $3.4 billion in late September to take over 1.7 gigawatts of heavy-duty Caterpillar gas generators that OnePWR Solutions had ordered about two years ago. The deal buys Anthropic OnePWR’s place in Caterpillar’s production queue plus engineering and installation services — but not OnePWR itself or its employees. OnePWR needed cash to finish paying for the order, and because large generators now carry multi-year waitlists, Anthropic effectively skipped the line by taking over the backorder. Piper Sandler called it a $3.4B sale of OnePWR’s power-generation platform to “a frontier AI lab” — the largest known single purchase of power-gen equipment by any AI lab. 1.7 GW is roughly 1.4 million homes’ worth of electricity. Caveat: the FT story rests on a single unnamed source, Reuters couldn’t verify it, and neither company has commented.

Field & industry developments

Voters reject data centers; AI companies offer concessions. This week’s US elections delivered a clear anti-data-center message: Alhambra, CA voted 83% to ban data centers; Wells, Maine voted 82% for a 180-day pause; Ballotpedia counted 46 of 47 local data-center ballot measures this year favoring tighter limits. The core concern is cost allocation — data centers can require new power lines and grid upgrades, and depending on how costs are split, nearby households may pay via utility bills. An October Senate investigation (Warren, Blumenthal, Van Hollen) found companies disputed how much they owe for shared-grid upgrades and continue using NDAs and seeking tax breaks. Two days after the midterms, OpenAI announced new commitments for towns hosting its future data centers: pay for electricity and infrastructure with financial guarantees so residents aren’t stuck if a project falls through; publish each site’s water/power use, noise studies, and local hiring data; stop requiring confidentiality agreements from public officials (except narrow security/proprietary cases); and give residents input while plans are still changeable, funding priorities they choose. OpenAI pledged $80M in Effingham County, GA and $40M in Pike County, OH. Caveat: the commitments cover only future sites OpenAI owns and operates — partner-owned capacity (e.g., Oracle) isn’t automatically covered, and enforceability is unclear. Microsoft pledged in January to cover its data centers’ electricity infrastructure and stop seeking lower property-tax rates; Amazon’s “Built Together” promises >$1B over five years for local priorities.

China’s “Stargate” in Inner Mongolia. SemiAnalysis reported that the first 120MW phase of China’s planned 800MW “Galaxy” AI campus in Inner Mongolia was delivered in August at about one-fifth of comparable US cost.

Jobs data counters the “AI is wiping out white-collar work” narrative. US professional and business services added 70,000 jobs in October — the largest monthly increase since July 2022 — a surprise gain some economists read as evidence AI isn’t erasing white-collar work.

AI super PACs underwhelm. AI super PACs spent ~$79M across 68 races this cycle; the OpenAI-linked “Leading the Future” ended with just over $20M of its roughly $100M.

US AI Safety center drifts toward the White House. Per AP, the US government’s AI safety testing center began informally reporting to a White House office and still has no permanent leader.

Claude Code’s “Don’t Ask Again” leaked 2,539 secrets. Security firm Apiiro reports that Claude Code’s “Yes, and don’t ask again” permission saves the full approved command — including passwords, API keys, tokens — in plaintext to .claude/settings.local.json, which is not added to .gitignore. A scan of 104,185 public copies on GitHub found real credentials in 1,122 files and 2,539 distinct secrets across 1,994 repositories, including 379 database passwords, 23 AWS keys, 42 GitHub/GitLab/npm tokens, and 43 SSH logins. Apiiro says four related reports to Anthropic were auto-closed by a bot with no reply, and half the exposed files sat on default branches for at least 217 days.

Founder & business strategy

AI gross margins, pricing, and solo founders. A “7 ways to handle your AI gross margin problem” piece (an investor in 700+ companies) advises charging for a unit your product controls (e.g., a personalized AI-SDR email) rather than raw tokens — while still tracking the token cost behind each unit, since equal-revenue actions can cost wildly different amounts; Vanta reportedly absorbs its agent’s token costs at lower margins to drive adoption. CNBC profiled “no-employee” AI-era small businesses (one owner expecting $2.5–3M across 12 brands), with preliminary research showing rising business applications without hiring indicators in AI-exposed industries. Related essays: “The rise of the one-person institution” and “Symbiotic firms built around humans and AI.” “Why wouldn’t the labs do it?” argues AI labs now threaten startups broadly (models cover most knowledge work), pushing ventures toward physical operations, proprietary data, or regulated workflows — Patrick Collison separately questioned what a positive AI future looks like and warned that granting moral worth to vast numbers of AIs could overwhelm human interests under utilitarianism. A VC analysis of 158 SaaS IPOs found sales-and-marketing efficiency does not improve with scale (median $0.71 → $0.66 of new revenue per S&M dollar from the year before listing to the listing year; Palantir listed at $0.33, Snowflake $0.57).
