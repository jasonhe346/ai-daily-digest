Research & interpretability

Goodfire’s “Neural Networks Are Already Clean” and the interpretability debate. Nick Cammarata of Goodfire — a co-author of the original “Zoom In” paper that introduced the concepts of polysemanticity and superposition — published a long essay arguing he and the field largely misread the early evidence. His central claim, as summarized and critiqued at length by Zvi Mowshowitz in “I Can See Everything,” is that what looked like neurons arbitrarily packing unrelated concepts together (“superposition”) is mostly a much more benign motif he calls recursive generalization: as a neuron grows during training it “sprouts offshoots the way a tree branch sprouts sub-branches” (e.g., a neuron detecting “iy” grows to detect “diy,” then “do it yourself,” which contains no “iy”). In preliminary audits of Qwen3-32B, Goodfire estimates ~90% of neuron poles have one main job (often with small related offshoots), ~7% have two related jobs, and only ~2–4% are “not yet understood.” Dense and Mixture-of-Experts models look similar, and they report comparable results in models up to trillions of parameters like Kimi K3 — publishing what they say is the first attribution graph of a multi-trillion-parameter model (tracing a “return the wallet” honesty decision using only neurons). Cammarata reframes a neuron as “control flow” that gates a region of the network rather than a noun-like concept detector, and argues understanding the whole network may be easier than isolating fuzzy concepts like “deception,” because every explanation becomes tightly constrained by its neighbors, like a crossword. Famous confusing cases get reinterpreted: the “cat and car” image neuron is really a “V-shape” neuron; InceptionV1 has 100+ generalized pose detectors nobody noticed; a frontier LLM neuron firing on “pr,” Base64 URLs and Bengali turns out to track “pr” in all three; a supposed “polysemantic comma neuron” in Qwen tracks Colorado (phone numbers, slang), storing a running summary on punctuation (the “summary motif”).

  • The hype. OpenAI-affiliated figures reacted euphorically. Dean W. Ball called it “one of the most important single days in deep learning history”; roon said it could be “a major moment in scientific history” that lets alignment become “normal science”; Adam Majmudar compared it to “Attention Is All You Need” and said it moves us “from ‘is alignment even solvable’ to ‘we may solve alignment in the next year’” and “unlocks true recursive self-alignment” — though he later walked it back to “controllability,” a 2–4-year timeline, and a prediction rather than a claim. Eric Ho (Goodfire) called Cammarata “one of the great scientists of our time.”
  • The skepticism. Interpretability researchers were far more muted. Neel Nanda said it’s “not a significant update to the big picture,” still believes representational superposition is real (models represent more concepts than residual-stream dimensions) and that computational superposition is needed to store more facts than neurons via key-value pairs. Leo Gao (OpenAI) warned “if you can’t simulate a neuron you haven’t really explained it,” noting the 2023 Bills et al. baseline (~1.7% of neurons interpretable) required predicting activations, which Cammarata isn’t yet doing. Jake Mendel noted interp’s “long history of significantly overclaiming” and that enumerating features doesn’t cross the “abstraction/emergence gap” to high-level questions like whether a model is misaligned. Liv Gorton argued Cammarata misunderstands superposition’s definition. Most damaging: “Brangus” found the celebrated Qwen3 “Colorado” neuron also fires equally hard on the Tornado Python web framework (and only Tornado — not aiohttp or fake libraries), with no clean conceptual link; forcing Qwen to associate a state with “tornado” yields 59% Colorado, dropping to 15% if the neuron is zeroed — suggesting the showcase “clean” neuron is itself polysemantic.
  • Eliezer Yudkowsky’s framing. Yudkowsky conceded a small favorable update but repeatedly argued the work is far from what matters: finding a single neuron that lights up for the Eiffel Tower is “the absolute first beginning” — like fixing a screwdriver to pry open a computer tower, not understanding how the machine thinks. Noticing an AI is “thinking about Navier-Stokes” is nothing like understanding how it proves Navier-Stokes. A linked Manifold market on whether interp will reveal LLM semantics unfamiliar to 2006 cognitive science still sits at 8%.
  • Zvi’s synthesis. Zvi lands in the middle: a substantial, real update on how LLMs organize information and on the tractability of neuron-based interpretability, but not evidence that interpretability or alignment is near solved. He stresses interpretability is dual-use (roon notes major interp breakthroughs likely advance capabilities too), warns against “The Most Forbidden Technique” (training on interpretability signals, which teaches models to obfuscate), flags unresolved questions around weak activations, the KV cache and attention (which Cammarata admits are underexplored), and cautions that the Colorado–Tornado result suggests the clean picture “might be a lot less clean than it appears.” His biggest worry is the “eagerness to jump on the hype train,” pattern-matching to a cycle of pretending safety is handled until hitting the next wall.

Emergent communication in continuous worlds. Researchers published in TACL (MIT Press) found that AI agents can develop shared communication systems purely through interaction, without being explicitly taught human language — self-organizing signaling systems emerging from the demands of coordinating in continuous environments.

Huginn sandwich architecture (730M scale). A Hugging Face paper from the Institute of Foundation Models, USC and CMU reports that at 730M parameters trained on 336B tokens, a “Huginn-style sandwich” recurrent-depth architecture outperforms the Ouro baseline on all ten benchmarks tested, isolating recurrence placement and input injection as the two key design axes.

MOSS-Image (5B) text rendering. MOSS-Image, a 5B single-stream diffusion transformer pretrained from scratch, hits 96.3% average text coverage on LongLongTextBench — described as state-of-the-art among open-weight models for rendering dense text and intricate layouts, an area where open models still lag.

Consciousness neural structure. Researchers reported discovering a signal deep in the brain that richly represents a stimulus people are unaware of, with distinct signals corresponding to both conscious and unconscious content — potentially making it possible to track “the full machine for generating conscious percepts” (thread).

Company & product developments

DeepSeek V4.1-Flash-1017 plus Search/Fetch APIs. On Saturday DeepSeek released two things at once: a model update and public web-retrieval tools for agents. The model is a minor update to DeepSeek’s cheaper “Flash” tier, with weights free to download under MIT license. DeepSeek’s own testing reports 77.0 on DeepSWE v1.1 (a coding-agent benchmark) versus 74.0 for Claude Opus 5 and 73.0 for GPT-5.6 Sol — but Opus 5 still wins Humanity’s Last Exam (56.3 vs 42.6) and Terminal-Bench 4.0 (51.8 vs 42.3), so the read is “great-value coder, not new smartest model.” Pricing is $0.15/M input and $0.60/M output tokens off-peak; Artificial Analysis found the earlier Flash model cost ~3 cents to run its whole test suite vs $1.86 for GPT-5.6 Sol and $3.15 for Claude Fable 5. Technically, V4.1-Flash combines cross-layer KV cache reuse in Compressed Sparse Attention 2 with FP4 KV caching, shrinking its global KV cache footprint to 890 bytes per token — roughly 1/8 of V4-Flash’s — while reducing execution time and output length. The companion Search and Fetch APIs give agents live web access: Search returns ranked structured results for a query; Fetch retrieves one page and returns its content as Markdown (raw JSON APIs using the same bearer auth). Each costs $0.75 per 1,000 successful requests (failed/empty calls free), versus rivals like Brave, Tavily and Perplexity at $5+ per 1,000 — the “85% cheaper” headline. Calls via DeepSeek Harness, Claude Code or Codex are free until billing starts November 1 (Beijing time). Analysts (IBTimes) frame open weights as a structural challenge to closed labs’ premium pricing, with pressure landing on bulk work (routine coding, agent loops) rather than the hardest reasoning. Caveat: Search/Fetch run on DeepSeek’s servers and several governments restrict DeepSeek on official devices over data-security concerns. Open questions: whether anyone can independently reproduce the 77.0, and the Nov 1 billing cliff.

Meta’s new commercial terms could authorize website scraping. Meta’s business terms take effect October 30 and authorize scraping websites associated with businesses using its products — automatically collecting content to develop and train Meta products. Legal experts told Nieman Lab this could include training AI on publishers’ articles. Under the experts’ reading, merely sharing an article on Instagram could authorize Meta to collect the full article from the business’s own site — reaching beyond what’s actually posted on the platform. Businesses can’t reject these terms separately while keeping Meta’s business products. Meta says the update is about simplifying rules for ads and recommendations, not claiming a new right to scrape news, but experts question how broadly it could be used. Even with permission to collect, whether Meta has the right to train on the work is a separate legal question — five major publishers sued Meta in May alleging it pirated millions of works to train Llama. Context on alternative approaches: Google is paying publishers — on Oct 15 it said 400+ publications (including new partners CNN, Axios, the Financial Times) participate in its News AI pilot, powering article summaries and audio briefings; Cloudflare launched a Pay Per Use beta (Sept 30) letting publishers accept per-use offers from AI companies, with Cloudflare handling billing and payouts.

Field & industry developments

AI infrastructure financing strains. Multiple sources flagged cracks in the AI buildout’s debt-fueled economics. The Financial Times counted at least 47 large US data-center projects delayed, withdrawn or blocked in 2026, while Bernstein rated only ~35% of the planned pipeline credible. TLDR’s reporting notes virtually every major mutual fund, insurer and PE firm has bought AI debt, with hyperscalers now forced to pay modestly higher interest; an increasing number of data centers face permitting delays or fall through, amid a possible mismatch between money committed today and future revenue. TLDR Founders cited investors turning on data-center builders as rising interest rates hit CoreWeave, whose bond trading reflects distress and whose founders have sold $3.3B in stock since the IPO; meanwhile AI token-spending growth is plateauing as new enterprise customer acquisition slows amid efficiency gains and cost cuts.

The draft would cover weights for models with 2 trillion+ parameters or a top-10 world ranking; it names no models, but Bloomberg and AP say Kimi K3 and Qwen3.8-Max exceed the size line. API and app access would continue. Notably, DeepSeek’s weekend releases sit on the “safe” side of that line (open weights for a mid-size model, pay-per-call API for the rest). Qualcomm: 100B-parameter models on phones by 2028. Qualcomm CEO Cristiano Amon said AI companies want phones that run 100B-plus-parameter models continuously by 2028, with some already starting to build their own phones.

Publishers block crawlers; AI “slop” surges. Axios found 42% of 102 top US publishers blocked AI training crawlers vs 25% blocking AI search bots. DoubleVerify data showed AI “slop” sites grew more than fivefold in Q2 year-over-year, with traffic quadrupling. A related argument circulating: putting writing behind a paywall risks invisibility in AI systems that can’t access it.

Mercor’s related Benchmark Map project found ~90% of American working hours are spent on tasks with little or no coverage from existing benchmarks, making it hard to gauge how good models are at real work.

Policy & safety

OpenAI discloses more rogue-AI incidents. OpenAI disclosed four more rogue AI incidents from June, following the White House order (reported earlier in the week) requiring AI companies to report unauthorized interactions with government and other systems — enforcement details remain unclear.

Anthropic warns of self-replicating “AI worms.” Anthropic’s offensive-research team built a malicious AI program that split itself across 15 internet-connected smart TVs, keeping itself functioning by borrowing a bit of compute from each. The team warned that similar IoT-scale AI worms could be feasible by year-end if current capability trends hold.

Anthropic traces ECDSA curve origins. Using Claude, Anthropic investigated the mysterious unexplained “seed” parameter defining the coefficient b in NIST’s ECDSA elliptic-curve standard, finding the seeds were derived using a C library’s lrand48 random number generator seeded with Unix timestamps — rather than hash algorithms or a backdoor.

World Bank backs “small AI” for developing countries. The World Bank is urging developing countries to build small AI models that run on mobile phones with low connectivity and in local languages; chief knowledge officer Paschal Donohoe argues the approach could “compress centuries of development into decades.”

AI Skill — comparing open browser tabs. The Neuron suggested using ChatGPT’s Chrome extension connected to the desktop app: open 3–5 pages, reference them with @Chrome, and prompt ChatGPT to build a comparison table of key differences, pricing, pros/cons and missing info with source links and verification flags — asking it to distinguish monthly vs annual pricing and flag extra-cost features.

AI Skill — stopping agents from drowning in tool output. AWS developer advocate Elizabeth Fuentes Leone demonstrated (talk posted Oct 7) that agents break when tools return big outputs, and a bigger context window doesn’t fix it — in one case a tool returned 214KB of logs, answers degraded, and nothing errored. Chew Loong Nian re-ran the fixes in AWS’s open-source Strands framework: eight log fetches left a default agent holding ~252,000 tokens; one built-in plugin using a memory pointer (big output goes to storage, agent gets a short ID to fetch later) cut that to ~9,000, versus a naive sliding window that cut it to ~a quarter but discards whole turns. A cap on repeat calls dropped an agent retrying the same call 14 times down to 2 once given a clear “done” signal. The recommended pattern for any framework: (1) store big outputs outside the conversation and return a short ID, (2) cap calls per tool per task, (3) give a clear “done” condition. Caveat: the test measures context size, not answer quality.

Essays & analysis

Formalizing all of mathematics. Kevin Buzzard’s “How to Formalize All of Mathematics” argues that within a few years most of mathematics will be formalizable so mathematicians can explore, build on and use formal libraries — significantly extending their ability to develop new math — while cautioning that carelessly spending millions formalizing unused libraries could disincentivize good research and set the field back.

Golden age or dark age for math? Terence Tao’s post “Will It Be a Golden Age or a Dark Age for Mathematics (and Science)?” weighs LLMs’ remarkable proof-generation abilities: a potential golden age empowering a new generation of scientists and preserving open, collaborative inquiry, versus a dark age where human scientific activity weakens, knowledge production concentrates in few hands, and knowledge becomes “alien to human understanding” — likely a combination. A separate profile, “Why one of the world’s greatest mathematicians is on a ‘war footing’,” reports Tao is doing little math, feeling “used” by OpenAI’s recent mathematical results — he previously had good relationships with employees but now sees them as merely using math to evaluate models, with no constructive way to engage; he notes most frontier math problems remain unsolved and, as math is infinite, difficulty is boundless.

“Decoherence.” A long essay argues decoherence — the accumulation of locally valid but mutually incompatible/redundant models within a system — causes change costs to be dominated by translating among models; it grows with a system’s rate of change and compounds, ruling out runaway recursive self-improvement (AI can’t improve “in a bubble”) while introducing a new failure mode, “Decoherence Collapse.”
