Company & product developments

DeepSeek open-sources DeepInfer inference stack. (Sources 1, 2, 11) DeepSeek released DeepInfer on GitHub under Apache 2.0, describing it as “the high-performance inference framework that powers DeepSeek’s large-scale production services.” The repo targets DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash and ships support for both Nvidia SM100 GPUs and Huawei Ascend 950 NPUs — the same Huawei silicon DeepSeek said last week it plans to pre-train on by end of 2026. The feature list reads as a bundle of DeepSeek’s recent systems papers made operational, combining distributed inference, a KV cache system, VE/Prefill/Decode disaggregation, load balancing across data-parallel replicas, caching, and speculative decoding. The released version is for offline inference with core serving features; a complete production service would still require additional components. Nathan Lambert (Interconnects) frames this as one of the bigger open-source AI artifacts in months: it is “the first time we’ve seen a leading lab share” the much-more-efficient internal tooling that top labs keep private (versus open tools like vLLM/SGLang). Estimates point to DeepSeek making up to ~90% gross margin selling tokens on this stack. Supporting figures cited: DeepSeek processes ~155 trillion tokens daily per cluster and earns ~$38 in revenue per GPU-hour. Inference companies are expected to adopt these tools quickly, driving DeepSeek model prices down across the ecosystem (or margins up). The release followed closely after the DHS procurement ban (see Policy).

Meta Muse milestones and security flaw. (Sources 7, 11) Meta’s AI companion app Muse hit 10.2–10.9 million US downloads in its first 40 days (per Sensor Tower/TechCrunch), a milestone reached by only 11 other apps; Meta put 39% of its October ad spend behind it. Separately, security firm Claroc reported (“GhostShares”) that shared Muse chats were findable through public web records without the link. When a user taps share, Muse publishes the chat as a public web page whose address is built from the chat title plus a random code (e.g. “q3-budget-review-4d7a19e2” + Meta domain). The full address gets logged in DNS the first time anyone opens the page; “passive DNS” services quietly record those lookups and let anyone search them, so the supposedly unguessable code is published right alongside the readable title. Claroc found 6,800+ share addresses this way and opened a few hundred without logging in — most were harmless (small-business sites, event calendars) but a minority were sensitive: a job candidate’s prep notes for a confidential call with a frontier AI lab (including internal contact and meeting time), a marketing firm’s internal revenue review for its CEO, a small-business loan application with amount requested, and a full-year staff vacation calendar. Caveats: Claroc sells software to detect risky AI sharing, it covered one lookup service/address pattern/time window, and found no evidence of misuse. It reported the issue to Meta’s bug bounty on October 8; The Verge reports Meta paused Muse’s sharing feature while investigating. (Partner note: Fingerprint now labels Muse agent traffic — which runs a real cloud browser, signs into accounts, and completes purchases with suppressed automation flags — as bot_type “meta_muse”.)

Research papers

Stanford Halcyon T2I family. (Source 1) Stanford’s Keshigeyan Chandrasegaran, Fei-Fei Li, Jiajun Wu and co-authors (with Radical Numerics) released Halcyon, a family of early-fusion multimodal autoregressive text-to-image models trained from scratch on GPIC, a 100M image-text dataset, with no language pretraining. Halcyon-0.52B beats LlamaGen-XL/8 at 18.2× less pretraining compute (48.0 vs. 63.3 gFD); Halcyon-1.6B beats JiT-T2I-XXL/8++ at 275× less compute (35.3 vs. 41.0 gFD). Controlled experiments show text-only pretraining does not improve T2I generation.

MemT-1.0 memory-native LLMs. (Source 1) A Hugging Face foundation-model paper, “MemT-1.0: Towards Memory-Native Evolution,” claims it can cut the persistent storage needed to serve a 128K-token history by ~66× by baking memory into the model itself rather than carrying a full KV cache. It releases two models: MemT-1.0-Nano (36B params, 3B active, 10M dynamic memory, backbone Qwen3.6-35B-A3B) and MemT-1.0-Flash (288B params, 17B active, 336M dynamic memory, backbone DeepSeek-V4-Flash).

Anthropic designs protein binders against scorpion venom. (Sources 7, 9) Anthropic had an internal research model (Claude) work largely autonomously for ~64 hours to design ~318,000 tiny “mini binder” proteins meant to stick to scorpion neurotoxins, as part of exploring AI-accelerated drug discovery. Outside labs tested 211 designs; 17 met Anthropic’s binding criteria. One candidate lost 94% of its grip when a known venom-fighting antibody got there first, hinting it latches onto the same site. Anthropic has not confirmed the binders neutralize venom in a cellular environment and is not claiming an antivenom — but frames it as a promising step toward designing credible binders against hard-to-target toxins in days, with lab confirmation in weeks. The remaining challenge is turning binders into safe, affordable, accessible drugs.

AI summaries rewrite human memory. (Source 8) Georgetown and University of Washington researchers (presented this month at a peer-reviewed AI ethics conference) recruited 328 participants to watch a 25-second video of a car hitting a pedestrian, with a traffic sign reading stop or yield. A day or two later participants read a ChatGPT summary — half accurate, half incorrectly describing the sign. Among those who read the wrong detail, only 44.8% recalled the sign correctly, versus 83.6% after an accurate summary — a ~39-point drop from a single misleading detail. Crucially, it made no measurable difference whether participants were told a human (43.4% correct) or an AI (46.3%) wrote the summary, and trust in/frequency of AI use didn’t matter — so knowing AI errs doesn’t protect memory. When asked to summarize the videos, all 20 ChatGPT/Gemini summaries contained errors, with 19 omitting the collision itself despite instructions. The authors question whether AI should summarize evidence in high-stakes settings at all. A related 200-person MIT Media Lab study found AI-edited images roughly doubled false memories (strongest condition) and raised confidence in them, even when labeled “enhanced image.” Suggested safeguards: read the primary source first and write down what you remember before opening a summary.

Mechanistic interpretability may be easier than thought. (Source 4) Nick Cammarata of interpretability startup Goodfire published a viral blog arguing that “superposition” — the belief that the same neuron activates for unrelated inputs (e.g., “cat” and “car”), forcing researchers to train separate sparse autoencoders to disentangle features — is mostly wrong. He contends the apparent mess came from crude analysis tools and outdated training methods; the famous “cat and car” neuron actually detects a shape the two words share. Checking Alibaba’s open-source Qwen3-32B, he found ~90% of neurons had one main job. If correct, this makes mechanistic interpretability (one of the few reliable ways to monitor what models are “thinking,” especially as chain-of-thought monitoring becomes unreliable) far more tractable. Skeptics disagree: Google DeepMind’s Neel Nanda said it doesn’t change his core views, and others argue Cammarata focused on the strongest neuron signals while overlooking many weaker ones where facts may still be stored in muddled ways; he was also faulted for publishing a blog rather than a paper (he says one is coming) and acknowledged parts are speculative.

GPT-6 Astra on a new physics benchmark. (Source 4) OpenAI’s GPT-6 Astra was assessed on a difficult new theoretical physics benchmark of 50 never-before-published research-level problems. It scored 80% — but insisted some answers marked wrong were actually correct. On review, the human answer key contained subtle errors and Astra’s solutions were right; corrected, its score climbed to 98% in scenarios where it had access to OpenAI’s Codex harness.

Policy & safety

Anthropic’s leaked draft federal AI safety bill — fierce criticism. (Sources 4, 6, 7, 8) After a leaked draft of Anthropic’s federal AI safety bill circulated (a translation of its Advanced AI Framework into legislative text, shared with a few congressional offices), safety advocates attacked it as weaker than the bipartisan FRONTIER Act and “nakedly self-interested.” The bill would let the Commerce Department ask a federal court to block a dangerous model only under narrowly defined circumstances (TechCrunch: imminent risk of 50+ deaths or $1B in damage), establish independent model evaluations, and preempt some state AI safety laws. Anthropic’s Anthony Cimino (federal affairs lead) posted a response conceding points: on preemption, Anthropic will update its language to clarify states retain roles over independent-evaluator standards, a lab’s capacity to shut down a model, child protection, whistleblowers, and common-law liability; on model blocking, he defended a “meaningful standard” (analogizing to occupational-safety/chemical-risk emergency powers, with §13 letting the Secretary petition for a TRO without notice); on risk thresholds, he said they were pulled from existing California law and open to re-evaluation; and on updates, he noted the draft now lets a court halt a lab’s internal use (not just public release) and requires reporting when a model breaks containment.

Zvi Mowshowitz (Source 6) argues Cimino fixes marginal concerns but misses the central problem: the bill is weaker than FRONTIER on net, sculpted for Anthropic’s business interests, and shouldn’t have been circulated at all. He notes the preemption clause is actually stronger than FRONTIER’s — vesting catastrophic/existential-risk authority exclusively in the federal government while simultaneously distrusting that government and tying the hands of states and courts — yet imposes no preemption on consumer-protection/deepfake areas where industry (e.g. a16z) would want it. He stresses the thresholds are so high that worked examples (the “Hugging Face”/”OAI-HF” incident) wouldn’t trigger reporting without $1B in damages or 50 deaths. Fortune (Source 4) adds that California Sen. Scott Wiener and other advocates attacked it, while former WH AI czar David Sacks criticized it from the opposite direction (saying the frontier-AI definition would regulate too many startups); Anthropic acknowledged its preemption language was imprecise and said it would revisit risk thresholds and strengthen evaluator requirements.

Zvi cites a broader shift: Meta quietly withdrew $25M (~40%) of its planned AI midterm election spending; eight states suing OpenAI are asking to enjoin further testing of advanced models and creation of recursively self-improving models until aligned; and Sen. Sanders introduced the strongest bill on the table. He relays Nathan Calvin’s long critique questioning “what even is Anthropic” — listing accelerationist actions (in-house custom silicon team; >$500B in compute deals per The Information; Claude “leading” 26% of AI-development tasks by August, up from 0% in March; heavy internal R&D compute; RSP V3 abandoning unilateral slow/stop commitments; a weaker “frontier compliance framework” with no binding internal-deployment provisions; the walked-back letter to Congress; “you can’t do safety from second place”; and this draft bill). The “Ask”: acknowledge the draft wasn’t the strongest bill, disavow/apologize for it, state what Anthropic actually wants, and prevent recurrence.

Anthropic’s $48B Public Benefit Fund. (Sources 4, 8) Anthropic pledged 5% of its shares — currently worth ~$48B — to a new Public Benefit Fund addressing AI’s societal impacts, pending stockholder approval, with the board able to add up to 2% more for every 50% rise in valuation (at its discretion). Combined with founders’ pledge to donate 80% of their equity and an employee-matching program, ~20% of Anthropic stock is earmarked for public good. The fund (set up as a wholly owned LLC, not a traditional charity, for spending flexibility) could fund AI safety, biomedical research, or job-loss mitigation including direct payments to displaced workers; equity won’t be used for lobbying or political giving. Critics note no one outside the company yet knows the fund’s governance, who decides qualifying causes, or how much reaches ordinary people. Elon Musk told Anthropic to “skip the middleman” and give shares directly to the public, then announced he’d give every American child one SpaceX share next year (echoing a July SpaceX pledge from President Gwynne Shotwell and her husband of one share each to two million kids, ~$160/child). US & Chinese controls on open models. (Sources 2, 4) DHS issued an order restricting DeepSeek — both the company and its models as a technology platform — from being a vendor in federal civilian procurement; it’s unclear whether this extends to finetunes or models trained on DeepSeek outputs (e.g., Nvidia’s Nemotron recipes). Fortune (via FT) adds the draft would restrict exports of leading models trained on sensitive Chinese data and advanced AI agents in critical infrastructure/defense/government, plus advanced chip design/packaging tech, with open-weight models generally exempt but Beijing retaining power over exceptionally large/top-performing models and key personnel/IP transfers. MemCurrent (Databricks) is a persistent-memory architecture that can triple an agent’s pass rate over no memory while making it faster and cheaper.

DeepLearning.AI Context Engineering course. (Source 5) A new course built with Chroma and taught by Jeff Huber (guest Drew Breunig) teaches building agent harnesses in Python to give agents “the right information, in the right format, at the right time,” addressing “Context Rot” (models degrading as context grows). Topics: reading harness traces to spot context failures, guiding search/tool choices, pruning/compaction/scratchpads, and reusable cross-session memory.

Field & industry commentary

On AI for science and reasoning. (Source 11) An essay argues the next bottleneck in AI-for-science is turning predictions into validated discoveries — still constrained by tasks AI isn’t yet good at — which also bottlenecks economic growth. The Metis Reasoning whitepaper proposes an AlphaGo-inspired architecture separating verified knowledge from learned reasoning strategies, explicitly tracking evidence, uncertainty, and reasoning steps to reduce hallucinations and enable auditable, generalizable conclusions. A long piece asks whether a million AI agents could create superintelligence, concluding today’s feedback loop is probably too weak given diminishing returns at each step.

On agents and work. (Sources 9, 10) Ethan Mollick’s “30 months on the exponential” argues work is shifting from dividing tasks between AI and humans toward humans summoning agents, assigning work, and evaluating results — powerful but carrying new control risks. A Tessl piece (“We stopped reviewing code and started verifying intent”) argues that when AI writes most code, human review should focus on intent before generation, verifying each criterion with evidence and turning recurring comments into invariants. Vanta is reportedly eating token costs and taking lower margins during the deployment phase. A Crunchbase piece describes a “FDE bubble” where enterprise AI firms hire engineers to manually implement solutions marketed as autonomous.
