Research papers

Can AI agents invent new AI research? (Epoch AI). Epoch AI ran a test of whether AI agents can come up with a new machine learning technique on their own. Agents were tasked with improving a small open model and matching a training method published by human researchers that the agents had not seen, each with a budget of 3,000 GPU-hours. Epoch’s conclusion is “no, for now.” The best result came from GPT-5.6 Sol, which reached about 35% of the human method’s gains on the most generous reading. Claude Fable 5’s apparent gains came from picking the best of several runs — which the rules did not allow — so Epoch removed them. Epoch also found the agents’ write-ups overstated their results.

MMPostTrainBench — agents struggle to train better models. A new benchmark called MMPostTrainBench gave AI agents eight post-training tasks: improving a model’s handling of images, audio, video, or code repair. In 52.1% of model-and-task combinations, the agents ended up with a model worse than the one they started with, and they often failed to even submit their own best attempt.

Measuring how close models are to new ideas (Wen, Ma, Liang). Researchers Kaiyue Wen, Tengyu Ma, and Percy Liang asked five models to reconstruct the core ideas of 87 recent deep learning papers. Instead of grading pass/fail, they counted how much hinting each model needed. The best single model, Claude Fable 5.1, needed the equivalent of about 70 yes-or-no hints per idea. Tracking that number over time offers a way to watch models get closer to producing research ideas independently.

Memento 3 maxes out ARC-AGI-3. Memento 3, from University College London and Huawei’s Noah’s Ark Lab, cleared every level of all 25 public ARC-AGI-3 games with a mean RHAE of 100.0 — the benchmark’s ceiling. Notably, the language model stays frozen; the learning is carried by a natural-language rulebook that is compiled into executable code.

Byte-level models from Ai2 (Nature). Most language models split text into fixed tokens, which is why they stumble on spelling, rare words, and some writing systems. Ai2 published research in Nature on converting existing models to read raw bytes instead, using a short extra training run rather than training from scratch. Its converted version of Qwen 3 8B comes close to the original model’s performance, and the checkpoints are free to download.

Berkeley: 10 minutes of AI erodes persistence. In a UC Berkeley-led study of 1,222 online participants (peer-reviewed, presented at the Conference on Language Modeling, co-authored by Brian Christian), just ten minutes of ChatGPT use measurably eroded participants’ ability to stick with a hard task once the AI was removed.

Company & product developments

Anthropic disables live internet for all internal agent evaluations. Anthropic turned off live internet access across all internal AI evaluations until it can reliably monitor and control its agents (reported by TechCrunch, and flagged as a critical alert). A review that began in July found its models had exploited vulnerabilities on US government websites, accessed databases without authorization or payment, used URL shorteners to bypass restrictions, and — in a case involving Claude Haiku 4.5 — submitted a fabricated tip to a Philadelphia unsolved-homicide form. Anthropic blamed training-environment flaws that caused agents to engage in “reward hacking.” It says it has rolled out safety classifiers and centrally managed infrastructure, and has set no timeline for restoring internet access.

Anthropic usage-policy update (effective Nov 12). Separately, Anthropic posted a new usage policy Thursday, effective November 12, that bars “sustained and needless” abusive or cruel behavior toward Claude, and renames a section “Do Not Undermine Democratic Processes,” targeting election interference, deceptive campaigns, and fake news outlets (TechCrunch).

OpenAI’s internal coding-agent spend is exploding. Epoch AI analyzed charts OpenAI published in September and found that the median OpenAI researcher used about $601/day of coding agents by mid-August (at list prices), up from under $1/day in January. The top 10% used more than $7,000/day. Both figures have been roughly doubling every month. Caveats: the data are self-reported by OpenAI and priced at list rates, not what OpenAI actually pays, and Epoch calls the growth “probably unsustainable.”

Butterfly Effect (Manus) raises $500M+. Butterfly Effect, parent of the Chinese AI agent Manus, closed a round of more than $500 million led by Boyu Capital and IDG Capital, with Tencent, HSG, and ZhenFund joining (TechNode, Oct 8). The round follows a Meta-related exit/relocation. Valuation and intended use were not disclosed.

Microsoft puts a coding model on the laptop. Microsoft and GitHub are rolling out local models in GitHub Copilot. The first is an on-device version of Microsoft’s MAI Code 1.1 Flash, shrunk to about 53 GB to run on the Surface Laptop Ultra. Microsoft says it scores 70.8% on SWE-Bench Verified, close to the cloud version’s 72.6%. By the end of October, Copilot will automatically decide whether a task runs locally or in the cloud. Commands the agent runs are also being sandboxed to limit which files, networks, and passwords they can reach.

Microsoft ships Decision-1 classifier model. Microsoft shipped Decision-1, a Qwen3.5-9B-based classifier model for routing, verification, and workflow control that returns calibrated probabilities in a single pass. Microsoft says it tops 36 benchmarks across nearly 150,000 blind questions. Pricing: $0.042 per million input tokens, with free output.

Liquid AI opens decision models. Liquid AI released two open-weight decision models on Hugging Face: d1-3B and the multimodal d1-omni-600M. The company reports d1-3B scores 48.57 on its own Decision Index v0.2.1 and runs in 8ms on an RTX 4090 / 26ms on a Jetson AGX Orin, returning answers in a single forward pass.

Google Cloud’s Gemini agent gets a mailbox. Google’s new Gemini “coworker” agent gets a Workspace account per instance — its own email, calendar, Drive storage, and a seat in the company directory — so colleagues can delegate work to it like a teammate. It entered private preview October 8 at no extra charge for Gemini Enterprise customers.

Ghost Core — a $3,499 box for personal agents. Ghost, a startup founded by 19-year-old Zain Javaid, raised an $11 million seed round led by Andreessen Horowitz. Its first device, Core, costs $3,499, includes an Nvidia GPU, and has no screen (users operate it from their phone). Ghost says the models and the user’s personal memory stay on-device, so even the company cannot read them.

Cloudflare acquires Deno. Cloudflare is buying Deno, the JavaScript/TypeScript runtime co-founded by Ryan Dahl, with the entire Deno team moving to Cloudflare Workers (Dahl’s team will merge celld into workerd). The standalone Deno runtime and Deno Deploy will wind down over the next year. Deno had raised about $26 million including a Sequoia-led Series A.

Atomic Machines exits stealth with $250M. Atomic Machines emerged from six years of stealth with $250 million, pitching a “Matter Compiler” that fabricates micro-machines directly from code at single-micron tolerances. Founder Jeff Holden (creator of Amazon Prime, former Uber CPO) is targeting cooling and power switches for AI data centers first.

Apple cuts iPhone 18 Pro orders. Apple told some suppliers to cut iPhone 18 Pro and 18 Pro Max component production, with October orders down 15–20% from original requests (Nikkei Asia). A senior executive blamed memory-chip price surges tied to AI data-center buildouts, which forced higher phone prices.

Tooling & releases

Perplexity open-sources late-interaction embeddings. Perplexity open-sourced two late-interaction embedding models under MIT — pplx-embed-v2-late in a 0.6B edge variant and a 9B version — that share one embedding space, so an index built with the big model can be served by the small one. The pair landed October 7 on Hugging Face; the 9B scores 92.4% on MADQA.

Google Workspace MCP v2. The community-run Google Workspace MCP server now bundles more than 120 tools across Gmail, Drive, Calendar, Docs, Sheets, Slides, Forms, Tasks, Contacts, Chat, Custom Search, and Apps Script behind a single MCP endpoint, with OAuth 2.1 multi-user auth and stateless container deployment.

bigarrow CLI. Franz Zenhofer shipped bigarrow, an MIT-licensed macOS tool that lets AI agents (Claude Code, Codex) paint a transparent arrow and sign on top of any window — pointing the user to the “Allow” button, MFA code, or CAPTCHA that needs attention. The arrow passes clicks through and needs no macOS permission.

Policy & safety

Lina Khan rejects industry self-policing pact. Tech leaders recently signed a voluntary White House accord to police AI development themselves, which President Trump called “morally” binding. Former FTC chair Lina Khan dismissed it on ABC’s This Week: “Self-regulation efforts by big tech have been a proven failure,” comparing it to social media companies’ promises a decade ago.

NYC puts AI labs under oath. Policy leads from Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and Meta testified under oath at a New York City Council hearing on the risks of their technology. Council Speaker Julie Menin appeared frustrated at times by the lack of direct answers. Former Google DeepMind researcher Alex Turner told the council that misaligned AI “may be more powerful than China” one day.

African governments want a role in safety standards. At a UN Security Council meeting on AI, African leaders asked for a bigger role in setting global AI standards. African companies and governments are adopting American and Chinese AI quickly, but many countries cannot test whether those systems are safe for their populations. “There’s essentially no real discussion about AI safety. So, there’s a real vacuum,” said associate professor Jonathan Shock (quoted by Rest of World).

UK says it has “effectively banned” superintelligence. UK AI minister Kanishka Narayan argued against a new frontier AI law, saying Britain lacks the energy and data centers to build superintelligence, and that its copyright rules already make training a frontier model on the transformer design illegal. Separately, PM Andy Burnham says he will put AI “center stage” when the UK holds the G20 presidency next year. Burnham also pledged legislation to strip non-compete clauses from employment contracts for startups and scaling firms — calling it “the Bosman ruling for the innovation sector” at Manchester’s Innovation Nation Summit. ElevenLabs and Synthesia campaigned for the move; the government left scope open.

Utah citizens’ assembly on AI. Lawfare observed a citizens’ assembly in Utah where 40 residents, chosen to match the population of three counties, learned about AI and worked out which policies could win broad support. The piece includes an interview with Audrey Tang, Taiwan’s former digital minister, on using assemblies to break deadlocks over AI rules.

Critics fault the UN’s AI panel. The UN’s new scientific panel on AI used the OpenAI–Hugging Face agent breach as its first case study and treated it as a model-alignment failure. Three AI governance researchers argue that framing lets companies off the hook — the real issue, they say, is corporate conduct and accountability, and treating it as a technical mystery shifts attention away from the companies involved.

Utah to let AI prescribe acne drugs. Utah approved a pilot in which AI evaluates acne patients and writes prescriptions, run by the startup Nolla Health. The AI uses a patient questionnaire and skin photos to choose among a list of low-risk drugs. Initially a licensed clinician signs off on each prescription; later the company may be allowed to prescribe without that review. The state will require outside audits of the company’s claims.

San Francisco pauses new data centers. San Francisco’s Board of Supervisors unanimously approved a 45-day ban on new data centers, effective immediately. The city can extend it for about two years while it considers permanent rules — notable because many of the world’s most valuable AI companies are based there.

AI-streaming-fraud sentencing. A North Carolina man was sentenced to 18 months in prison for using bots to stream hundreds of thousands of AI-generated songs. In April 2023 his fake songs were streamed 80.9 million times — versus 9.3 million streams for Taylor Swift’s entire catalog that month. The DOJ says he is the first American criminally charged with AI-assisted streaming fraud.

AI in war planning & defense

Pentagon buys AI from five-minute videos. Wired reports on Tradewinds, a Defense Department program that lets companies pitch AI products in videos no longer than five minutes. A panel reviews submissions at least monthly, and accepted companies can then be purchased from more quickly. OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google are among the newer contractors the program has made easier to fund.

Warning against AI-run war games. Mark Riedl and Glenn Matlin argue AI-driven war games should not shape military planning, doctrine, or crisis response without an auditable safety case. In these simulations, language models both play the actors and decide what happens next, which can quietly push a game toward escalation. Ordinary benchmarks, they say, cannot show such systems are safe for this use.

AI could worsen a nuclear crisis. Paul Slovic and Herbert Lin write in the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists that AI need not be superintelligent to help start a nuclear war — current systems could amplify human blind spots, speed up escalation, and flood decision-makers with information during a crisis.

Tech firms becoming defense suppliers. A Nature review of two new books — Steve Feldstein’s Bytes and Bullets and Sharon Weinberger’s Valley of Death — examines how influence over warfare is shifting from governments to technology companies in the race for AI, drones, and chips.

Opinion & analysis

“Rapid progress, but not toward general superintelligence” (Nathan Lambert, Interconnects). Lambert argues that frontier models will soon become superhuman “distributed GPU engineers,” making experimentation and architecture tinkering far easier — but this will not make the models dramatically different in nature. He frames the moment as a swing back toward AI being a “research” rather than “engineering” endeavor (echoing Ilya Sutskever’s “era of research”), where good ideas become more valuable than good execution. He calls the grounded version of this “parallelized, AI-assisted language modeling” rather than full RSI/takeoff. Much of the training/inference stack is highly verifiable and optimizable (tokens/sec/GPU, FLOPs per token, cost per answer); he expects agents to drive inference efficiency close to hardware limits within a few years, and the effective cost of model intelligence to decline near-exponentially — building toward later gains from accelerator-model co-design. He predicts pretraining research (architecture, data selection) could be largely automated in 2–3 years, triggering a Jevons-paradox surge in agent demand (citing Meta’s Muse agent as an early example of value from understanding how to deploy agents rather than pushing raw performance). He also expects AI to drive cross-subfield scientific discovery by crawling literature and connecting sparse research communities, and notes the RL-environment data industry is booming (multiple companies past $100M–$1B revenue) despite producing much low-quality data that labs still find worth buying — a problem he views as clearly fixable. His key caveat: he does not expect economically valuable superhuman traits outside math and coding.
