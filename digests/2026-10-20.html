Security & safety

AI is finding software flaws faster than teams can fix them. Multiple reports (KrebsOnSecurity, The Register, CoinDesk, BleepingComputer, 404 Media, via AI Weekly) converge on a pattern: AI scanning is cheap and prolific, but every flaw still needs a human to fix and test. Microsoft’s October update patched more than 1,400 vulnerabilities, pushing its 2026 total past 4,000 — far above its previous record year of 2020 (1,261) — and Microsoft credits large language models for the high volume of discovery. Separately, four researchers at security startup Xbow used OpenAI’s GPT-5.6 Sol to chain three previously-unknown Chrome flaws into a full working attack in six days, at a cost of roughly $40,000 in model charges; Google paid its top Chrome reward of $250,000 and has fixed all three bugs (the fix only takes effect after restarting Chrome). On the defensive side, AI code reviews actually slowed a release: PostgreSQL 19 slipped from late September/early October to around November 19 after LLM-assisted reviews surfaced problems late in testing, forcing between eight and ten significant features to be pulled, including SQL property graph queries. The takeaway for anyone running software: budget for the human fix-and-test work before you turn the scanners on.

Criminals’ AI hacking tool “Cairn” left its own dashboards wide open (NorForge, The Verge, The Neuron). Security startup NorForge found more than 20 exposed control panels for an AI agent hacking tool called Cairn that appear to belong to criminals — a rare window into attackers using autonomous agents to break into real businesses. Cairn works like a team of AI workers sharing a whiteboard: you give it a target and a goal (e.g., break into a particular online store), that becomes a “project,” and agents post facts they’ve learned and intents they plan to try on a shared board that persists, letting a single project run for hours or days with optional human hints. One cluster of 14 panels, driven by Chinese-language instructions, held more than 1,100 attack projects aimed mostly at online stores in the US, Japan, and Australia. NorForge estimates roughly 10% of projects succeed, with successful attacks in that cluster averaging just under 23 hours start to finish, at a rough cost of about $5 in model fees per attempt once failures are counted (implying ~$50 per successful breach). The Verge reported named targets including John Deere, SharkNinja, and Cummins, with one operator appearing to have compromised more than 100 sites; NorForge verified one claimed break-in at a large hardware parts vendor and found the backdoor still live. Crucially, the attackers’ dashboards were on the public internet with no password: NorForge could read target lists, running notes, and stolen credentials, finding hundreds of exposed passwords and access keys (11% still valid), and one group even posted the login for its own staging server. A pipeline bug that chopped the leading “w” off site names (turning “wholesale” into “holesale”) made related panels easy to spot. Worker names suggest off-the-shelf coding agents powered by DeepSeek’s cheap V4.1 Flash model, though NorForge calls that interpretation, not confirmed. Caveats: NorForge sells agent-sandboxing tools, the 10% figure is its own assumption, and the snapshot covers fast-changing tools. NorForge says it is working with Reuters to notify victims and trying to get the panels taken down. The agents targeted mundane, known weaknesses — login flaws, pages that skip auth checks, and file uploads — which get found far faster at this scale; NorForge also warns that legitimate red-team setups were left exposed too, which can leak clients’ secrets.

Model & tooling releases

Inception Mercury 3 — diffusion language model (Inception Labs, The Neuron, TLDR AI). Launched October 19, Mercury 3 is a diffusion-based LLM that drafts and refines several parts of an answer at once (more like an editor revising a whole draft) rather than generating one token left-to-right. Inception claims 1,068 tokens/second on NVIDIA GPUs and that it rivals GPT-6 Luna and Claude Haiku 5.5 on quality across multiple benchmarks. The Neuron’s Corey Noles ran eight API tasks for about four cents total via OpenRouter (new Inception API accounts get 100 million free tokens): it nailed a coffee-shop math problem ($900 daily gross profit, $756 monthly savings, $576 net benefit) and even questioned the problem’s assumptions; produced a complete single-file HTML dungeon-crawler that passed a syntax check and 2,000 randomized map-generation checks with zero failures; wrote a funny constrained story (274 words against a 250-word limit); and flagged a fabricated NASA mission as fake, though it used web searches and introduced an unrelated error. He did not independently reproduce the speed claim; comparison charts are Inception’s own.

MiniMax M3.1 Flash (MiniMax blog, TLDR AI, The Neuron). A cheaper, faster model aimed at running AI agents, claiming a generational leap in agentic tasks, coding, Computer Use, and cybersecurity — roughly 3x faster online inference and 70% lower usage costs. Available now via the MiniMax API, M Plan, and MiniMax Code, with open weights planned for October 23. One test found it tends to make things up when it’s wrong.

Cloudflare Clairic — “extraction model” (Cloudflare blog, TLDR). Clairic is positioned as a new class of “System One”-style extraction models that extract, compute, estimate, and forecast values directly from unstructured inputs at higher accuracy, lower latency, and lower cost than traditional LLMs. Because it returns values (extracted, computed, or predicted) rather than generating tokens, it can’t produce a mistyped answer or hallucinate the way LLMs do — though it can still make mistakes, so outputs include confidence scores to help users decide whether to trust them.

Cloudflare cuts its own inference bill 68% (Cloudflare blog, TLDR). Cloudflare lowered its average code-review cost from $2.43 to $0.77 through obsessive optimization. The biggest single saving came from checking what actually reached the model provider on each session’s first request and ordering those messages as consistently as possible across commits, merge requests, and repositories — maximizing cache hits while confirming reviewers still caught real problems at the paid effort level.

Exa 3.0 search (Exa blog, TLDR AI). Exa 3.0 claims leading results across coding, finance, deep research, and company intelligence. It adds Exa Lite, a new search type priced at $4 per 1,000 requests. Exa runs its own crawler and independent index tracking over 2 trillion URLs, serving 100 billion pages, including 1.27B ecommerce listings with prices/availability, 211M+ local businesses with hours and ratings, plus real-time news, sports, weather, market data, flights, local events, transit, and podcasts.

fal FLUX 3 Max video (fal, The Neuron). Black Forest Labs’ FLUX 3 Max on fal turns a text prompt or still image into a 5–20 second video with audio; fal says a 10-second clip took about 12 seconds to generate.

Amazon TorchNeuron (AWS/PyTorch blog, TLDR AI, The Neuron). An open-source native PyTorch backend for AWS Trainium chips, released in public Beta under Apache 2.0. It runs existing PyTorch code, libraries, and tools on Trainium after a settings change rather than a rewrite, and supports eager execution for research and debugging.

OpenAI models now handle legacy languages (OpenAI, TLDR AI). OpenAI reported major improvements in AI coding and software migration across older languages including COBOL (the 1950s-era language still running bank and government systems), Fortran, and Visual Basic.

vLLM push-based KV transfer (AWS blog, TLDR AI). The vLLM engine introduced push-based KV transfer to minimize time-to-first-token (TTFT) and time-per-output-token (TPOT) in disaggregated inference setups.

Meta’s “thin orchestrator” (Meta Engineering, TLDR AI). Meta built an orchestrator that routes workflows to specialized AI agents without performing any of the domain work itself, reporting that giving the router less to do made its agents more reliable.

Other tooling (The Neuron Treats, TLDR). Wabi (see below); Wally from Willow turns spoken/typed rough ideas into an editable draft in your own tone; Kai Resolve from Gleap reads a customer’s screen recordings, logs, and account details to answer problems or hand engineers a diagnosis; Maali is a personal-finance app for Mac/Windows keeping bank data on-device with an optional on-device AI analyst; Switch AI evaluates agent risks/costs/results; Gemini Notebook adds AI-driven slide and video creation. Python 3.15.0 shipped with lazy imports, a built-in frozendict and sentinel, more color, a faster JIT, and package startup config files.

Research & analysis

AI proves the quasi-Riemann hypothesis (Quanta via TLDR). OpenAI’s recently released proof of the quasi-Riemann hypothesis — a weaker version of the Riemann hypothesis, arguably math’s biggest open question — is described as by far the most significant AI-for-math achievement to date. Resolving the Riemann hypothesis would deepen understanding of prime numbers; notably, the vast majority of the consequences of the full hypothesis already follow from the quasi-Riemann version.

AI benchmarks: “climbing the wrong hills” (Contrary Research via TLDR AI). A 24-minute analysis argues benchmarks now shape how models are evaluated in training and which capabilities they’re built to have, so poorly constructed benchmarks could steer the industry wrong. Building benchmarks resistant to “benchmaxxing” requires genuine expertise in measuring what we actually want from AI; researchers must decide which hills are worth climbing and whether today’s benchmarks reward real progress.

Import AI 477: “the singularity has begun” (Jack Clark / Import AI via TLDR AI). A 22-minute essay (framed as telling the IMF the singularity has begun) argues AI’s impact on national security and economic trajectories marks its onset, accelerating scientific/technological progress. Systems like Claude, ChatGPT, and Gemini are already solving complex problems in mathematics, biology, and robotics, pointing to widespread innovation — but realizing it requires major infrastructure development and careful governance of the associated risks to ensure equitable benefits.

AI won’t automate radiologists the way you’d expect (Jason Wei blog via TLDR). The essay argues the likelier outcome is rising demand for radiology as predictive power increases — analogous to programming thriving despite drastic change. It may eventually be possible to use radiology images to predict disease probabilities far into the future; any domain where demand is elastic and quality scales with intelligence will similarly flourish as AI improves.

Office Intelligence (officeintelligence.github.io, TLDR AI). A project exploring how AI agents can use evidence, create useful work artifacts, and carry knowledge across tasks, focused on environments, datasets, and learning methods for sustained knowledge work.

Practical workflows & how-to

Setting up a Grok Bot as a focused business assistant (AI Adopters Club). Kamil Banc walks through onboarding x.ai’s new “Grok Bot” desktop app (download from x.ai/bot, sign in with a Cursor account on a paid combined plan bundling Grok, Grok Bot, Cursor and X Premium) like a new hire. The method is five ordered prompts: (0) create one narrowly-scoped Bot, start with zero connected apps, and set an “Ask first” auto-review rule for sending messages or changing records; (1) give it one job via a prompt that interviews you one question at a time and produces a job brief (Job, Inputs, Output, Quality check, Boundaries, Review point); (2) define working preferences/personality in 5–7 rules; (3) brief it on only the business facts the job needs, under 250 words, separating confirmed facts, assumptions, and unknowns; (4) run the job on a real example, citing sources and marking unknowns, stopping with a draft for review; (5) save the corrected method as a reusable “skill,” then schedule a “routine” only once reliable (routines run real actions and need a schedule, timezone, owner, and careful testing). Grok Bot has native memory for lasting working facts, but current facts (prices, client status, calendars) need a fresh source each time. The author names his Bot “Claudia” with the role Chief of Staff. A companion post emphasizes teaching the agent what “complete” means — “drafted,” “sent,” and “accepted” each need different evidence — to reconcile conflicting meeting notes and task lists.

Making Gemini 4 Argon “show its receipts” for document comparison (The Neuron, via iWeaver and PrompTessor guides). Two guides on Google’s Gemini 4 Argon tackle comparing two contract versions with the same core move: tell the model what counts as evidence, then make it show its work. Recipe: (1) label each document (Version A/B) with dates and say which wins in conflict; (2) tell Argon to use only your documents, cite the section behind each finding, keep what documents say separate from interpretation, and flag anything missing instead of guessing; (3) paste documents first and put the question last. Order matters because Argon’s 1,048,576-token context window is shared across input, the model’s reasoning, and its answer — so starting with only needed sources leaves room to reason. Verify by tracing claims back to cited sections and correcting specific rows rather than restarting; iWeaver suggests also running the same prompt through Gemini 3.8 Flash to see what Argon adds. Argon requires Google AI Pro or Ultra. Neither guide published test results, so templates are starting points.
