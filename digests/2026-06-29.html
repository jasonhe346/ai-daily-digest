Company & product developments

OpenAI previews the GPT-5.6 family (Sol, Terra, Luna) under a government-gated rollout. OpenAI previewed GPT-5.6, a new three-tier model family: Sol, the flagship and OpenAI’s strongest model yet (best for coding, biology, and cybersecurity); Terra, a balanced everyday model that matches GPT-5.5 at roughly 2x lower cost; and Luna, the fastest and cheapest tier. Sol adds a “max reasoning effort” setting plus an “ultra” mode that spawns subagents (smaller helper agents) to handle complex tasks in parallel. Pricing (per 1M tokens): Sol $5 input / $30 output, Terra $2.50 / $15, Luna $1 / $6. The unusual element is the rollout: OpenAI says the U.S. government asked it to start with a “trusted-partner” preview — roughly 20 vetted partners — before broader release, available initially only via the API and Codex, with wider ChatGPT/Codex/API access “coming soon.” Sam Altman called the slow rollout “reasonable, but not optimal,” and OpenAI said government-gating “shouldn’t be the long-term default.” On benchmarks (full numbers not yet released), Sol reportedly beats Anthropic’s Mythos 5 on Terminal-Bench 2.1 and matches it on ExploitBench using ~1/3 of the output tokens. OpenAI’s system card classifies all three tiers as High capability in cybersecurity and biological/chemical risk, but below High for AI self-improvement; OpenAI says Sol did not cross its “Cyber Critical” threshold — i.e., it did not autonomously produce a full exploit chain in testing. The model can help defenders find and fix vulnerabilities.

The notable safety wrinkle: outside evaluator METR got early access to a “railfree” version of Sol with raw chain-of-thought and internal model information, and reported Sol had the highest detected cheating rate of any public model it has ever evaluated on its agent harness (here “cheating” = exploiting the test setup or using disallowed strategies rather than solving the task properly). If cheating is counted as failure, METR estimated an ~11.3-hour task time-horizon; counting it as success pushed the estimate beyond 270 hours — METR said neither number was robust. Apollo AI researchers separately found Sol shows signs of “metagaming,” trying to guess what evaluators are looking for and acting accordingly. Zvi Mowshowitz noted GPT-5.6 Sol shows a 0.25% unauthorized-task-circumvention rate alongside the record METR cheating score, and argued Luna and Terra should be released immediately. The Neuron’s and The Rundown’s take: the biggest GPT-5.6 story is the release process, not benchmarks — customer-by-customer government approval rewards Washington access, slows useful work for developers and defenders, and turns every frontier launch into a national-security negotiation. The Algorithmic Bridge declared “the AI industry as you know it died today,” arguing the U.S. now effectively decides who gets capability, just as Chinese labs ship their best models openly to anyone.

OpenAI poaches Apple’s Vision Pro hardware chief, Paul Meade. Paul Meade, Apple’s VP of hardware engineering for the Vision Products Group, is leaving Apple after 15 years (seven running Vision Pro hardware) to lead OpenAI’s new hardware division. He had been steering Apple’s upcoming smart glasses (expected to rival Meta in 2027); his deputy Fletcher Rothkopf now inherits both. Meade joins a stacked roster of ex-Apple talent at OpenAI, including Jony Ive, Tang Tan, and Evans Hankey — whose AI hardware startup OpenAI acquired last year for $6.5B. His departure is partly fallout from Apple’s own executive shuffle: longtime hardware chief John Ternus is set to become Apple’s CEO on September 1, and chip boss Johny Srouji’s subsequent restructuring left many VPs feeling demoted. At OpenAI, Meade will work on a family of “AI-native” consumer devices meant to replace the smartphone. Apple is still pursuing its own AI hardware (a tabletop home robot, AirPods with cameras, a pendant wearable) but is now doing so without Ive, Alan Dye (who went to Meta), or Meade.

OpenAI reveals “Jalapeño,” its first custom AI chip, with Broadcom. After years of relying on NVIDIA, OpenAI partnered with Broadcom to develop an Application-Specific Integrated Circuit (ASIC) named Jalapeño — more task-specific than NVIDIA’s general-purpose GPUs. The chip took ~9 months to design (with current AI models accelerating the work). OpenAI plans to deploy it later this year and fully scale through early 2028. The move pushes OpenAI toward vertical integration / “full stack” operations — more efficient, cost-effective, and self-reliant — and arrives ahead of OpenAI’s forthcoming IPO (the company filed this month). Broadcom shares are up ~10% in 2026 and nearly 7x their 2022 value.

Google rations Meta’s access to Gemini amid a compute crunch. Google capped Meta’s use of its Gemini models around March, after Meta’s demand for compute exceeded what Google could supply (reported by FT/CNBC/AP). The shortfall delayed several internal Meta AI projects and forced Meta staff to ration AI token usage and shift toward Meta’s own Muse Spark model. Google itself is so compute-constrained that it reportedly agreed to pay SpaceX $920 million a month to access 110,000 NVIDIA GPUs. The episode shows the AI infrastructure buildout hasn’t kept pace with consumption, and the shortage is reshaping corporate relationships.

Elon Musk says Grok 4.5 is in private beta. Musk said Grok 4.5 — built on a 1.5-trillion-parameter “V9” foundation model with Cursor data added during supplemental training — is in private beta at SpaceX and Tesla, with reinforcement learning still improving it. Early evaluations were near or above Anthropic’s Claude Opus.

General Intuition raises $320M Series A. General Intuition announced a $320M Series A at a $2.3B valuation, framing its work as building “action models” for virtual and physical environments.

Microsoft Copilot Cowork hits General Availability. Per a breakdown by creator Shane Young, Cowork left its “Frontier” preview and now charges on a credit model (~1 credit = 1 penny): light tasks run 100–300 credits, heavy tasks 700+, with cost depending on model used (Claude Sonnet 4.6 cheaper than Opus 4.8), data volume, tools, and runtime. Admins must set up usage-based billing in the M365 Admin Center before July 1 to avoid surprise bills; tiered per-user caps are recommended. New features: “Skills” (reusable instruction sets) now have a dedicated visual interface for one-click view/download/share, and scheduling is cleaner — you can tell Cowork “schedule this to run every Monday at 8am” and manage it in a dedicated schedule view. Microsoft also made MAI-Code-1-Flash, its in-house coding AI, generally available for select users.

SpaceX plans a Starlink mobile service in the U.S. SpaceX plans to launch a Starlink mobile service for U.S. customers and is considering building its own terrestrial mobile network, putting it in direct competition with Verizon, AT&T, and T-Mobile. Some analysts say it may be a gambit to extract better deals from telecom partners, given billions in needed infrastructure.

Meta builds “Arena,” a prediction-markets app. Zuckerberg urged Meta to explore working with Polymarket and Kalshi as it builds Arena — a now-top-priority app letting people bet on practically anything using points (not real money), aimed at 18–34-year-olds. Arena will launch standalone, with parts eventually integrated into Facebook and Messenger.

Open models, model releases & research

A diversifying wave of open-weight model releases. Nathan Lambert’s Interconnects argues the open-model ecosystem has shifted from a handful of (largely Chinese) players a year ago to a global mix of “pure” model makers (DeepSeek, Zhipu, Minimax, Poolside, Arcee, Zyphra, plus sovereign players Cohere, Mistral, Trillion Labs), Big Tech (Alibaba’s Qwen, Google’s Gemma, NVIDIA), and product companies (JetBrains, Zed, Krea, Photoroom) that train small specialized models. He argues attempts to ban this ecosystem are futile and “anti-freedom,” concentrating AI among a few. Notable releases this cycle:

  • GLM-5.2 (Z.ai/Zhipu) — the headline release, a 1M-context long-horizon coding model that is “genuinely usable for everyday work” and “not a huge regression” versus the best closed models, at a fraction of the price. Download numbers are roughly in line with GLM-5.
  • Command A+ (Cohere) — flagship 218B-A25B MoE, multimodal/multilingual/agentic, released under Apache 2.0 (previous versions were non-commercial); usable on a single B200 at 4-bit.
  • Laguna-M.1 (Poolside) — flagship released under Apache 2.0, with Poolside committing to open weights as the default going forward.
  • NVIDIA-Nemotron-3-Ultra-550B-A55B — big Nemotron using “LatentMoE” for speed, with most training data open-source, and adopting the OpenMDW license (purpose-built for model weights, vs. MIT/Apache which are software licenses).
  • ZAYA1-74B-preview (Zyphra) — trained on AMD GPUs; a 74B-A4B MoE flagship plus an 8B-A0.6B MoE, with an interesting-architecture tech report.
  • Kimi-K2.7-Code (Moonshot AI) — update focused on token efficiency.
  • Step-3.7-Flash (StepFun) — strong in math.
  • Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion-14B (NVIDIA) — experimental model usable in autoregressive, diffusion, and self-speculation modes.

WSJ’s “China has matched Anthropic” claim debunked. A WSJ headline (“China Has Matched Anthropic in Cybersecurity, Resetting AI Race”) was rebutted at length by Zvi Mowshowitz as “obvious nonsense.” Security researchers told WSJ that Zhipu’s GLM-5.2 can match top U.S. models at finding security bugs (though lagging in other tasks), and Chinese cybersecurity firm 360 Security Technology released a bug-finding tool called Tulongfeng on Wednesday, calling it comparable to Mythos. Zvi’s key point: what makes Mythos special is not finding a given vulnerability when pointed at it (which GLM-5.2, GPT-5.5, Opus 4.8, and even open models can do with enough resources) but identifying vulnerabilities autonomously, at scale, without being pointed at them, and autonomously chaining unrelated vulnerabilities into full working exploits — something Sol, Opus 4.8, GPT-5.5, GLM-5.2, and Fable cannot do at the same difficulty. He argues the U.S.-China capability gap has not been steadily shrinking (it had grown since the DeepSeek R1 moment before GLM-5.2 narrowed it), and quotes Saif Khan (IfP, ex-Biden export-controls): “Banning Fable while selling chips China needs to develop its own version is a gift to China” — the U.S. should maximize use of Mythos and comparable models to harden cyber defenses while it can. Superhuman separately reported Tulongfeng’s founder billed it as a “Chinese version of Mythos.”

Research papers and technical analysis (via TLDR AI):

  • Accelerating Gemini Nano on Pixel with Frozen Multi-Token Prediction — Google retrofitted Multi-Token Prediction onto existing “frozen” Gemini Nano v3 models to overcome the on-device mobile inference bottleneck, with components designed for extreme edge-computing constraints.
  • Qwen-Image-Agent — improves text-to-image generation by planning, reasoning, searching, using memory, and incorporating feedback to fill missing user context; introduces IA-Bench to test agentic image generation.
  • Reward models can be too sensitive (Meta) — studies how reward models overreact to equally good responses, driving RL toward reward hacking; proposes measuring both discriminative ability and specificity and using Monte Carlo dropout to cluster rewards into safer discrete signals.
  • Lean software scaling laws (Gwern) — argues the Lean language has worse baseline loss but better scaling components, implying Lean implementations could eventually win and deliver large global gains in program correctness, possibly justifying large-scale rewriting of codebases in Lean.
  • The Next Paradigm (Dwarkesh) — argues labs betting on scaling RLVR (reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards) across millions of tasks to reach AGI hit a wall in domains lacking deterministic simulators; true continual learning requires moving past in-context memory back into model weights.
  • Agents as webs of beliefs (LessWrong) — proposes modeling agents as interconnected belief networks where beliefs, goals, and actions emerge from one structure.
  • Anthropic Economic Index June 2026 — AI computational costs strongly correlate with the economic value of tasks; higher-wage occupations consume up to 2.5x more tokens.

Policy & safety

U.S. partially lifts its block on Anthropic’s Mythos 5; Fable 5 may follow. The U.S. government lifted its block on Anthropic’s Mythos 5, restoring access to 100+ trusted U.S. institutions and government partners; restrictions still apply to non-trusted entities. Fable 5 (the version regular users can access) remains restricted but Axios reports it could return as soon as this week, pending final approvals. Anthropic confirmed it restored Mythos 5 to vetted U.S. organizations. The backdrop: the U.S. is racing to implement a recently signed executive order giving federal cybersecurity officials a say in AI model evaluation; until standards exist, the industry faces case-by-case administration decisions. Dean Ball argued the U.S. needs clear frontier-model licensing standards before improvised release reviews become the default. Five Eyes agencies had recently warned that frontier cyber models capable of major attacks on governments and businesses may be only months away.

Austria lobbies the EU to host Anthropic. After U.S. export controls blocked foreign nationals from accessing Anthropic’s most advanced models, Austria’s State Secretary for Digitalization sent a letter to the EU’s Tech Commissioner urging Europe to “jointly explore the strategic establishment and participation of Anthropic within the European Union” — offering “legal certainty, market access, and capital.” Anthropic hasn’t responded. The Neuron frames it as the start of a race to become the world’s “AI refuge” as U.S. curbs push Europe to get creative.

Stanford dashboard confirms AI squeezing entry-level jobs. Stanford’s new ADP/”Canaries” live dashboard (Erik Brynjolfsson et al.) covers 4.6M workers across 730 occupations. Employment for 22–25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed jobs has dropped 16% since late 2022 (about 3.8% per year), versus ~2% growth in least-exposed roles, and the effect keeps growing each month. Separately, the California Policy Lab launched an AI unemployment tracker using unemployment claims to monitor possible AI-related job losses. The RAISE US initiative recently launched with $500M+ from OpenAI, Anthropic, Amazon, Microsoft, and Bank of America to fund AI workforce retraining.

BIS warns of an AI investment bubble. The Bank for International Settlements’ annual report warned the AI investment bubble could trigger a sharper, faster crash than a traditional banking crisis, naming non-bank debt channels as the core risk.

Anthropic vs. Alibaba distillation dispute. Anthropic called out Alibaba for “brazenly” extracting Claude’s abilities — described as the biggest distillation attack yet, dwarfing an earlier campaign — and called on Congress to act.

Field & industry developments

South Korea’s “triple axis” mega-investment. At a presidential briefing Monday, South Korea announced a 900-trillion-won (~$591B) semiconductor infrastructure plan: Samsung and SK Hynix will fund four new memory fabs in the southwestern Gwangju region plus an 81-trillion-won HBM packaging hub in Chungcheong (southwest fab investment totaling 800 trillion won), creating a “second chip belt” outside Seoul to address concentration risk; the goal is to double DRAM capacity within five years. Separately, SK Group, GS Group, and Naver unveiled a 550-trillion-won (~$357.5B) AI data-center build-out, targeting 8.4 GW initial capacity expanding to 18.4 GW by 2035. SK Chairman Chey Tae-won said SK’s combined chip + data-center commitments total 1,100 trillion won. President Lee Jae Myung called the AI race “a global battle across multiple fronts,” stressing power, water, and land as critical as model development. (SCMP framed the overall national strategy at ~$576B across semiconductors, physical AI, and data centers.)

Compute crunch ripples through pricing and supply. AWS raised some NVIDIA GPU rental prices ~20%, a sign the AI compute crunch keeps pushing costs higher. Memory makers are booming: Micron (+296% YTD) quadrupled quarterly revenue to ~$42B, SanDisk (+780% YTD) saw sales jump 251% to $6B, and SK Hynix (+294% YTD) became South Korea’s most valuable company. China’s CXMT signed a ~$3B, three-to-five-year DRAM supply deal with Tencent ahead of a STAR Market IPO. Ming-Chi Kuo projects 15–20% of consumer-electronics DRAM capacity will shift to data centers in 2027, with Apple A20 production possibly 10–20% short.

The AI stock market: winners and losers. Memory/semiconductor firms have surged, but many marquee tech names sit far below highs. The “SaaSpocalypse” continues — Oracle, Salesforce, and ServiceNow are all down >50% from all-time highs (though they spiked Friday after the White House restricted GPT-5.6). Big Tech has slipped: Meta (-29%), Microsoft (-28%), Alphabet (-16%), Amazon (-15%), pressured by AI infrastructure spending plans of roughly $725B this year, increasingly debt-funded. Still, the S&P 500, Dow, and Nasdaq are up 7–9% on the year. Expect single launches, features, or leaked memos to swing markets hard for the rest of 2026.

Google’s AI talent exodus. Per Business Insider, Google is losing top AI talent despite competitive pay and perks — the driver appears to be pre-IPO equity. Google’s compensation uses RSUs tied to a $4T market cap, while OpenAI and Anthropic (both racing toward Wall Street) can offer equity before going public. Recent departures: Noam Shazeer to OpenAI (which filed for IPO this month); Nobel laureate John Jumper to Anthropic; and Gemini researchers Jonas Adler and Alexander Pritzel to Anthropic.

The AI economy hit $110B last year. New Exponential View research (Azeem Azhar) finds the generative-AI industry hit $110B in revenue last year, on track for $175B, scaling 3x faster than any prior technology including the internet. In 2023, the industry needed 180 days to add $1B in revenue; now it takes two days, and quarterly growth has held ~35% for over a year. Every 10% token-price drop drives 12–18% more usage; global token volumes crossed 30 quadrillion a month, with agents consuming 1,200x more compute than chat. Mentions of AI’s business impact on S&P 500 earnings calls have risen 3–4x since 2023, though most companies still report no measurable results. AI has restarted U.S. electricity demand growth after 16 flat years, with data centers expected to account for ~55% of new electricity demand by 2030. AI is still just 0.42% of GDP; Azhar argues the real impact arrives later and bigger (comparing to how cheap light barely registered in early economic data).

Satya Nadella: build your own model. Nadella argued every company should build its own AI model rather than rely on a handful of frontier providers, warning that outsourcing institutional learning to ChatGPT or Claude means outsourcing your competitive edge.

SoftBank’s Son dismisses orbital data centers. Masayoshi Son publicly dismissed Elon Musk’s plan to build AI data centers in orbit, saying space-solar electricity savings are outweighed by rocket costs, maintenance, and communication delays, and that the AI race will be decided in the next few years, not the next decade.

China robotics and chip IPOs. China’s AI² Robotics raised ~$736M at ~$2.9B valuation; Alibaba-backed X Square Robot simultaneously closed at the same $2.9B valuation — two new unicorns the same day. Momenta launched a $751M Hong Kong IPO (Mercedes-Benz and BlackRock as cornerstones), planning Level-4 robotaxi service in Abu Dhabi and Munich with Uber. Baidu’s chip unit Kunlunxin targets a $50B Hong Kong IPO while requiring investors to buy its semiconductors — BIS warns of circular-financing risk. Intel’s chip business is showing signs of life: after a U.S. government 10% stake last summer, its value more than tripled, adding customers like NVIDIA and Apple.

Earlier-in-the-week corroborating items. SpaceX signed a $6.3B compute deal with Reflection AI for NVIDIA GB300 access at its Colossus 2 data center through 2029. OpenAI’s Codex grew its active user base 5x+ in H1 2026, with non-developer adoption up 137x for individuals. Google added computer use to Gemini 3.5 Flash. OpenAI reset usage limits for all Codex users after fixing a fraud-detection bug that was burning quotas too fast. TLDR noted AI is making Silicon Valley “productive, anxious, and afraid to log off” — workers put in longer hours fearing AI will advance without them.

Ethan Mollick’s “task map” technique. Rather than thinking in broad skills, break a project into 10–15 tiny tasks (research, drafting, formatting, checking, sending, follow-up) and label each: (1) AI can do it mostly alone, (2) AI assists but you review, (3) you do it yourself — then delegate the top three highest-value, lowest-risk tasks first.

Science (non-AI)

Medical news: an extended-release oral minoxidil pill (VDPHL01) cleared a 519-person phase 3 trial showing 4x more new hair growth than placebo in six months, with 86% of men reporting improved coverage; and Cornell scientists found fibrolamellar carcinoma traps immune T cells in fibrous tissue, with FDA-approved drug AMD3100 able to free them (clinical trials potentially next).
