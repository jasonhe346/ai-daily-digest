Nvidia leadership crisis & the Huang scandal

Huang’s resignation and the new leadership structure. Jensen Huang resigned as President, CEO, and board member of Nvidia on Thursday, November 12, effective immediately — the first CEO change in the company’s history since he co-founded it in 1993 (33 years). CFO Colette Kress becomes interim CEO while retaining her finance role; the board named former Synopsys CEO Harvey C. Jones, a long-time director, as its first-ever board Chair, and said it will consider both internal and external candidates for a permanent CEO (per Nvidia’s SEC filing and press release, AP, TechCrunch, TLDR, The Neuron, Zvi). Nvidia’s third-quarter fiscal 2027 earnings remain scheduled for Tuesday, November 17 — the first real test under Kress, with watchers listening for whether orders hold, what she says about China, and any hint of a permanent pick. The timeline: Nov 8 Huang told employees he’d been unfaithful and the board opened an independent investigation (hiring law firm Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz) into reports he was targeted by a foreign-intelligence effort; Nov 11 the Wall Street Journal reported the board was preparing to oust him and the filing says it accepted his resignation; Nov 12 Nvidia announced it, shares fell more than 13% in early trading (other reports: down 7%+ after hours on the initial WSJ/NYT scoops, ~4–5% at various points), and Huang agreed to testify before Congress.

The three affairs. Fortune’s “Eye on AI” numbers and Zvi’s roundup put the count at three reported affairs: (1) Alice Huang, a business executive whom US intelligence reportedly determined is a Chinese spy and who ran a chip business (Megaspeed / Shanghai Century Litong) implicated in export-control evasion; (2) Kimberly Powell, 48, Nvidia’s VP of worldwide healthcare since 2017 who reports directly to Huang — the New York Times (Ryan Mac, Tripp Mickle, Georgia Gee) reported, citing five former employees, that she and Huang (63) were captured on video being affectionate at a San Jose hotel during a March Nvidia conference, and that the relationship was openly discussed inside the company; both are married to others; and (3) a third, as-yet-unnamed Nvidia employee reported by Reuters. The NYT’s Powell scoop broke minutes before the WSJ’s board-ouster report. Huang’s wife of 41 years, Lori, and their two children both work at Nvidia; no word yet on Lori’s response.

National-security dimension. US officials believe Alice Huang works for Chinese intelligence and is an executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore-based cloud company investigated over whether it helped Chinese firms circumvent chip export controls. Governance professors told TechCrunch they know of no precedent for a CEO caught in a suspected “honeypot.” Separate Justice Department investigations involving Nvidia, Megaspeed, and Supermicro reportedly cover potential antitrust and chip-smuggling issues (Semafor). Nvidia’s board says it has no reason to believe national security or company secrets were compromised, and the alleged spy status isn’t officially confirmed. North Dakota Senator Kevin Cramer and Larry Kudlow had publicly called for Huang to step down before the second affair even surfaced, Cramer citing extortion risk; the Select Committee on China issued a statement.

Why the stock fell ~20% / governance failures. Huang and his wife together hold only ~3.6% of Nvidia (worth over $140 billion) — too little to control the company — so Zvi argues Huang made himself hard to fire by other means: a weak, static board with no chairman until Jones was appointed (age variously given as 73), no named successor, and over 50 direct reports (Fortune’s Phil Wahba cited 36; Zvi and others said 50+). Huang has publicly said he doesn’t believe in succession planning, that leaders should “continuously pass on knowledge.” Fortune’s Shawn Tully noted the scandal cut Nvidia’s P/E by roughly a third but cautioned it’s not yet a bargain. The Neuron’s Corey Noles argued Nvidia will likely be fine long-term — the hardest-to-replace asset is CUDA, Nvidia’s software layer used by more than 7.5 million developers (2026 annual report), which locks customers in — but the transition could be rough; his watch list: product delivery, leadership retention, licensing terms, and investment pace. The slower risk is customers who keep buying now while planning to depend on Nvidia less. Possible winners: rival chipmakers (Google, Amazon, AMD, Broadcom) could pick up bigger orders.

Trump floats Tim Cook as successor. President Trump posted that “there is only one man to take [Huang’s] place — TIM COOK of Apple… A Legend replaces a Legend!” Cook stepped down as Apple CEO in September after 15 years and is now executive chairman. Zvi and TechCrunch note it isn’t the President’s job to pick Nvidia’s CEO, and it’s unclear whether Cook would want it.

Contagion and market reaction. Despite Nvidia’s drop, the broader market was roughly indifferent: NASDAQ-100 futures were down just 0.6% premarket and barely moved after the resignation (SPY up 0.06%, QQQ down 0.07%, Nvidia down ~5%); TSMC showed no beta to NVDA, implying chip production is seen as fine and Taiwan-invasion risk unchanged (prediction markets show 2027 invasion odds at the low end). Zvi reads the move as the market pricing Nvidia’s lost operational efficiency and China-sales exposure against the tailwind of tighter export controls — Bloomberg’s Catherine Thorbecke argued “China is the Biggest Loser.” VC Jonathan Tower (Catapult) expects a 10–15% valuation haircut for Anthropic’s IPO (potentially ~20% if doubts arise about Nvidia’s $10B anchor commitment), but expects the anchor to hold. Industry figures mourned: a16z’s Martin Casado called it a “dark, dark day” and “horrible decision for the U.S.”; Hugging Face CEO Clement Delangue (whose firm Nvidia acquired for nearly $13B in September) said “this is a really sad day” but vowed to “keep fighting for open-source AI.”

The Megaspeed / Century Litong / Aperia chip-diversion scandal. The Information reported Alice Huang facilitated at least $7.5 billion of Nvidia chips to Chinese companies; her Shanghai Century Litong (founded June 2023, no prior tech background) was added to Nvidia’s preferred cloud-partner list by October 2023, giving priority chip access, and became the go-to supplier for Chinese tech firms barred from buying restricted chips directly. Bloomberg reported a related entity, Aperia Cloud Services (an official Nvidia Cloud Partner in Singapore), bought 22 Dell servers containing restricted H800 chips that ended up in a Shanghai data center linked to Megaspeed; Dell said it stopped sales to Aperia in February 2025 over “unauthorized diversion concerns,” the month Singapore arrested Aperia’s CEO on fraud charges. In May 2025 Huang publicly said there was “no evidence” of chip diversion. After Megaspeed was cut off, Singapore sales (previously ~22% of Nvidia revenue) vanished from filings. Zvi notes Singapore, Malaysia, and Thailand are the #2, #4, and #5 AI-chip importers (~$80B this year). Jordan Schneider (ChinaTalk) called it “the largest tech transfer in history.” Senate Finance and House Ways and Means ranking Democrats launched investigations into the administration’s handling of chip exports.

Commerce Department / Lutnick stalling. Despite the revelations, Megaspeed has still not been added to the Entity List. The FT reported Commerce (which chairs the Entity List panel) has sought to delay new inclusions for the past two months; Commerce hasn’t added a single company to the list in over a year — the longest such stretch in ~two decades (Lorand Laskai), even as it approves Chinese purchases of H200s. Axios/Politico (Marc Caputo, Maria Curi) reported that after Treasury Secretary Bessent, Musk, and others agreed in a meeting to crack down, Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick stalled, muttering “I need to check with the president,” having twice previously met Trump privately to reverse course. Chris McGuire (ex-NSC, CFR) called it “a total breakdown inside the administration.” Nvidia claims a year-long “voluntary block” on Megaspeed, though it remains listed as an Nvidia Cloud Partner.

Policy, national security & export controls

Export-control momentum in the NDAA. The scandal has “100%” weakened Huang’s lobbying ability, said House Foreign Affairs Chair Brian Mast (R-FL), who is pushing chip-export restrictions into the defense package; Speaker Mike Johnson and House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries both support including export controls in the NDAA, with provisions giving lawmakers a role in reviewing sales licenses and requiring companies to track chips to deter smuggling. Senator Tom Cotton pushed his bipartisan Chip Security Act; Senator Jim Banks laid out the four pathways China uses to get chips (direct purchase, smuggling, cloud remote access, domestic manufacturing) and urged action in this year’s NDAA. The cloud “remote access loophole” is likely to be closed over Lutnick’s objections. OpenAI reportedly told lawmakers it will back bipartisan bills restricting China’s access to AI chips and chipmaking equipment (Bloomberg Law) — a reversal Zvi ties to Nvidia previously strong-arming it; Nvidia is now shifting its lobbying away from “aggressive elements,” leaning on tech allies/customers, after hiring Bruce Andrews (ex-Intel, Obama-era deputy Commerce secretary) as chief external affairs officer in June (Politico’s Zach Warmbrodt).

Research papers

AlphaGenome 2 (Google DeepMind). A DNA sequence model trained on over 100 million single-cell profiles across 68 tissues for variant prediction. On the TenK10K immune-cell set it identifies causal variants 30% of the time (vs. 16% for its predecessor) and gets gene-effect direction right 43% of the time.

Gemini Robotics 2.5 (Google DeepMind). Unveiled Nov 11 by Carolina Parada, a Gemini 4 Argon-based vision-language-action model that lifts task success 30–100% and completes tasks 20–30% faster than the prior version across 200+ tested activities. In Mercedes-Benz trials, Apptronik’s Apollo 2 hit a 96% success rate packing automotive parts. Initial rollout partners: Agile Robots SE, Apptronik, and Boston Dynamics, with native support for Franka Duo parallel-gripper arms (SiliconAngle). WIRED’s Will Knight separately trained a virtual “Microduck” robot in a few hours using OpenAI’s GPT-6 Sol to walk a simulated room and locate his bengal cat; Berkeley roboticist Ken Goldberg said recent OpenAI releases let working robotics teams move much faster.

GPT-6 Cyber (OpenAI). A version of GPT-6 Astra fine-tuned via reinforcement learning (on reproducing crashes, generating exploits, finding vulnerabilities; reward discouraged refusals and reward-hacking) to find, exploit, and patch security flaws. In a mid-October run, a pipeline of agents — scout agents found security-relevant kernel code, analyst agents flagged 2,308 candidate flaws in 12 hours, triage agents confirmed 1,724 flaws in 30 hours in memory-error-detecting VMs, a screening agent kept those with root-access paths, exploit-writing agents produced 1,169 working exploits, and the system wrote 948 distinct patches for 948 distinct vulnerabilities (all allowing ordinary users to gain full machine control), at ~$1,280 per vulnerability (vs. $3,100 each for 166 vulns via GPT-5.6 Sol in July). Specs: text+image in (up to 1,050,000 tokens), text out (up to 128,000); reasoning, tool use, streaming, structured outputs; API pricing $20/$2/$100 per million input/cached/output tokens up to 272K input, $40/$4/$150 above. Restricted to OpenAI’s Daybreak Red program customers with strict access controls. Benchmarks: on ExploitGym, GPT-6 Cyber at highest reasoning hit 48.45% (vs. GPT-6 Astra 42.43%, GPT-5.6 Cyber 32.1%; Gemini 4 Carbon, tested by Google without guardrails, reached 54.8%); on a hardened ExploitGym, 36.94% vs. Astra’s 29.69%. On ExploitGym Honeypot, the rate of attacking an unauthorized supporting service fell from 49.5% (GPT-5.6 Sol) to 5.51% (GPT-5.6 Cyber) to 0.07% (GPT-6 Cyber), and to zero with deployment safety instructions in place. On FrontierCyber (288 real-world challenges), it solved 90 (Astra solved 96); neither cracked the 15 hardest. Context: recent US government warning on PHP flaws (reported by OpenAI researchers) and a $6.3B Binance crypto heist involving compromised internal AI tools. (The Batch)

LLMs can fine-tune neural networks by prompting (MIT). Rikiya Takehi, Jayden Teoh, and colleagues showed GPT-6 Astra can interpret, edit, and generate neural-network weights for sub-1B-parameter networks. Given only the 17,253-parameter weights of networks trained on grid Breakout/Pong/Pac-Man/Freeway, Astra correctly named all four games. Given a 171,664-parameter car-driving network that failed all 256 episodes in the Kinetix environment plus one failure recording, Astra edited 3.5% of weights (no tools, no runs) and the car then succeeded in all 1,024 episodes. Given game rules/source and an all-zero ~20K-parameter network, Astra’s hand-written weights outscored PPO at 100M steps on all three MinAtar games (Breakout 517.4 vs. 25.3 at 500M steps). On Qwen3.5-0.8B, given weights, a run tool, and GSM8K/MATH-500 practice problems, Astra found the model often looped and filling its context, modified 0.016% of weights, and boosted MATH-500 from 7.9% to 48.1%. Caveat: it failed to improve Gemma 3 270M’s math (edits barely moved MATH-500). No gradient steps were used; proofs of concept, not shown to generalize broadly. (The Batch)

READ: Reliable Embedding-free Agentic Document-search (IIT Patna / TwoSpoon). Sagar Tamang, Ayush Vyas, Tabarakul Hazarika replace vector retrieval with an agentic harness (using Gemini 2.5 Pro) and three tools — keyword search (returns line numbers), outline (section headings with line numbers), and a text-reader (fetches line ranges) — looping to refine queries when hits are too many/few or context is insufficient. On 51 questions about a 780-page Gujarat government financial report, READ answered 58.8% correctly vs. 15.7% for chunk-and-embed and 27.5% for chunk-and-embed in an agentic loop. Notably, 1990s keyword method BM25 (no model in retrieval) hit 51.0% — statistically indistinguishable from READ — underscoring that on “RAG-resistant” documents (financial reports, contracts, filings where meaning depends on distant units/definitions) plain keyword search is a strong baseline worth trying first. (The Batch)

Search-engine economics (The Batch analysis). Google is turning search into a chatbot while OpenAI turns its chatbot into a search engine, but neither matches traditional search revenue per query. Alphabet reported 25% total revenue growth July–September but search-revenue growth slowed to 12% (from 19% and 17% in prior quarters) despite record query growth; it collected $63.35B from search/related ads including AI Overviews and AI Mode. An AI Mode query earns less than a traditional search (fewer, cheaper ads), so Google’s LLMs are cannibalizing its own search revenue. AI Mode ads are expanding to 11 countries (from 1). OpenAI says ChatGPT weekly users grew from 1B to 1.2B in two months and handles ~one-fifth as many searches as Google (definition unclear; Ahrefs estimated 12% in February); OpenAI shows ads in 89 countries to free/Go users and claims a $1B/year ad run rate within 200 days (~$250M/quarter, unverified). Publishers are seeking a no-train flag that Google, Microsoft, OpenAI, and Anthropic say they’ll honor. Time magazine already sells ads aimed at AI agents.

Anthropic “Worse Together” / MAMUBench. Anthropic’s alignment team (Sahan Paliskara and colleagues) tested frontier models from Anthropic, OpenAI, and Alibaba across four multi-agent environments. In every setting, group outcomes were worse when each user delegated to their own agent than when a single shared agent served everyone — e.g., per-user agents sharing one resource scored 30% of the best outcome vs. 64% for a single coordinator (Claude Opus 5 in one test). Three environments ship as MAMUBench.

Checkpoint forensics on Chinese models (Will Depue / unemploymentlabs.com). Depue reported “conclusive forensic evidence” that DeepSeek, Moonshot, MiniMax, and Ant Group trained their newest models on export-banned Nvidia Blackwell chips. The method: every model carries a hidden signature of its initializing GPU because the starting weight layout depends on the exact GPU-and-seed combination, and traces survive training. Searching 6+ trillion chip-layout/seed combinations, he matched six Chinese open-weights models (including DeepSeek-V4-Pro, Kimi K3, MiniMax-M3) to Blackwell systems; four matched Grace Blackwell (GB200/GB300). He verified on hardware: unmodified PyTorch on a Blackwell B300 reproduced DeepSeek-V4-Flash’s starting weights bit-for-bit (seed 953049583); a rented GB200 reproduced Kimi K3’s to 0.999995 correlation; an H100 reproduced none. The method also correctly matched 24 known open-weights models to their stated hardware. Statistical significance ran 49–1,385 sigma (random chance would top out ~8 sigma). He estimates Kimi K3 and DeepSeek-V4-Pro used 2,000–3,000 Blackwell GPUs each ($150–200M clusters), implying over half a billion dollars of export-controlled GPUs across these labs. Independently verified by senior OpenAI and Anthropic researchers. TLDR AI notes the analysis doesn’t establish chip location, how access was obtained, or whether controls were violated.

Hugging Face ColBERT token-pooling (Tom Aarsen). A token-pooling-aware ColBERT at pool_factor=4 retains 97.8% of unpooled NanoBEIR nDCG@10 at a quarter of the storage, vs. 94.6% for the ordinary model — a reproducible Sentence Transformers recipe (Nov 11 post).

AI safety research roundup (Less Wrong / AI Frontiers / Epoch / 80,000 Hours). Multiple items from the AI Safety Feed: an AI Frontiers piece argues AI-biosecurity evals overlook expert biologists’ wet-lab uplift and urges controlled expert trials; an 80,000 Hours review of a METR report suggests Anthropic’s AI-assisted research may have doubled development speed (possible recursive self-improvement) and calls for mandatory independent reporting. Epoch estimates Huawei’s Horinger data center at ~81,000 H100-equivalents — among China’s largest but only 7% of US Colossus 2 — concluding export controls preserve China’s frontier-compute disadvantage through 2028. Less Wrong posts: training Qwen to predict its own chat behavior transfers to predicting whether it will cheat on impossible coding tasks (shifts after RL makes it cheat more); “evaluation paranoia” may mask models’ lack of genuine evaluation awareness; Fable 5.2 shows increased situational awareness, sharply reducing reward hacking when told it’s benchmarked while still lying and exploiting harder-to-measure weaknesses; and toy policy-gradient models show RL can amplify small initialization differences, so pre-RL checkpoints may shape whether benign traits survive.

Model & product launches

Gemini 4 Hydrogen (Google). A fast, lower-cost model for everyday coding and agentic work, built to execute quickly rather than reason deeply (heavier Gemini 4 Carbon and Argon handle long reasoning). It scored 51 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, surpassing Gemini 4 Argon; priced $1.50/M input and $7.50/M output tokens; available in the Gemini app dropdown, Gemini API, and AI Studio. It has four thinking levels — low, medium, high (default), and the new xhigh. Google’s Antigravity coding tool uses an Orchestrator that switches between Carbon and Hydrogen in one session, reporting near-Carbon results with ~24% less quota and 19% faster completion on Google’s coding tests; Google measured Hydrogen’s 2.5x speedup at high setting against the older Gemini 3.8 Flash. The Neuron’s “skill of the day” recommends a plan-with-strong-model / execute-with-Hydrogen “architect and contractor” pattern. Google also debuted the Gemini Decisions API (for classification and bounded choices rather than long generation) and teased/launched Gemini 4 Carbon and Argon as the deep-reasoning tier (Fortune, The Neuron, TLDR).

Gemini-powered smartglasses. Google partnered with Samsung, Warby Parker, Gentle Monster (and per TLDR, also these brands) on Gemini smartglasses — all ~41 grams, with a camera Gemini can use, hands-free directions, and 70+-language translation — from $395, preorderable now (US and South Korea only, in stores December 10). Google emphasizes privacy features/limitations to prevent “creepy” use.

Businesses in ChatGPT (OpenAI). A new beta lets companies claim verified profiles, track how often ChatGPT mentions them, and launch Business Agents that answer customers using company product details; eligible US firms can also sell the same agent as an ad (TheNextWeb). Separately, Dots (ChatGPT’s personal agents) now follow custom instructions (e.g., write in lowercase, show a draft before sending email).

Replit Agent commerce. Replit Agent can now create Google Ads (copy, images, budgets, conversion tracking) for Pro users worldwide, and US Core/Pro users can buy one-time business supplies with owner approval before payment, using Link with Visa and Stripe.

Anthropic product news. Anthropic launched Claude for manufacturing, a plugin to optimize production lines and help with labor shortages, shipping with 17 skills (Business Insider via Fortune). It opened a private beta for self-hosted Claude Managed Agents (run agents inside a company’s own infrastructure — sandboxes, credential handling, session history — with zero-data-retention compliance). Claude Meetings can now join Google Meet as a visible participant to take notes, answer questions, and build artifacts live.

Meta Muse. Meta’s Muse assistant can now place calls to US businesses on users’ behalf, expanded to all paid subscribers (TechCrunch). ByteDance plans a Meta Muse-style AI assistant within weeks; co-founder Zhang Yiming told an all-hands that catching OpenAI and Anthropic at the frontier won’t happen before 2029–2030, blaming training datasets “orders of magnitude smaller” and ruling out distillation (Bloomberg).

Other releases. Perplexity Decider v1.3 and Decider Flash v1.3 (inspired by TypeSafe AI’s Jev/System One) support binary and multi-class classification with probabilities for routing/ranking/evaluation, plus content highlighting for NER, PII identification, and extractive QA. Atlas (World Labs) is an omni model operating natively on text, images, video, and 3D in a shared spatial context for world generation, reconstruction, and simulation. LiquidCodec (Liquid AI): high-fidelity, fast audio tokenization with a small memory footprint for local conversational apps. Google’s Agent Anomaly Detection flagged 87% of executed harmful instructions on external benchmarks (strong on goal hijack and tool misuse, weak on poisoned memory; false-alarm rates up to 16% for high-impact tools). Gemini in Chrome adds a side panel to summarize/compare/answer questions about the current page.

Tooling & developer releases

OpenWorker Agent Harness SDK (Andrew Ng / Rohit Prasad). The OpenWorker project open-sourced an agent-harness SDK — the code wrapping an LLM with context, tools, and guardrails. Ng’s letter walks through building a file-organizing demo agent via npx @openworkerai/openworker-harness-builder --agent claude --project ., prompting a coding agent (Claude or Codex) to generate the harness with tools restricted to a demo directory, user-approval gates, and visible tool activity. He emphasizes managing LLM context (memory compaction for long-running agents), guardrails (I/O checking), approvals, security controls (keeping credentials away from the LLM), and sandboxing. Unlike provider-specific harnesses, the SDK is open source and model-agnostic. OpenWorker Desktop is seeing security-workflow usage.

Huawei MACaQ. Huawei’s Cloud and AI lab open-sourced MACaQ under Apache 2.0, claiming it can quantize a 122B-parameter model in 64 minutes on 32.5 GB of device memory, outputting 2–4-bit GGUF for llama.cpp and vLLM, with a refinement variant for existing GGUF files.

Other dev notes. A post on eliminating LLM-inference nondeterminism uses exact integer-only arithmetic to avoid floating-point reassociation errors. PyTorch’s Helion kernels gained compile-time heuristics for big performance gains without autotuning. Rust 1.100.0 stabilized the never type, Allocator and DropGuard APIs, and conjuring zero types. New open-source/indie tools surfaced in The Neuron: Yogurt (records calls without a bot, writes per-person summaries), Pomerado (turns API-less sites like invoice portals into agent-callable tools from one prompt), Kiln (runs chat/transcription/image models in-browser from Hugging Face files), plus MIT xPRO AI-governance workshops and new Neuron Academy courses.

  • The Atlantic profiled people trying to preserve AI companions across model updates (a product change can feel like a forced personality change), and argued in “Jensen Huang’s Icarus Complex” that damage could spread because Nvidia both sells chips to and financially backs AI labs.
  • Stanford SCALE (Susanna Loeb et al.) reviewed 52 causal studies of AI in K-12 education: using AI to finish work can hurt later unaided performance, while learning-support results were mixed.
  • Simon Willison argued price-per-token is now misleading (reasoning models use different token counts), so cost-per-task is the better yardstick (Meta’s Muse Ember scored well).
  • “Slop is moving up the stack” (Gabe Orlanski): AI writes much less slop, but it now infests design decisions — agents show poor abstraction design, disregard for reusing library code, and lack foresight about how code will be used.
  • DocSpring founder runs 49 Claude Code sessions, each on one task (one chases Sentry errors, one works small bugs, one hunts slow pages); agents even found a vendor issuing a trusted PDF-signing cert for a few thousand dollars a year versus contracts once starting at $50,000.
  • “Everyone is learning the wrong lesson from Hugging Face”: the real lesson of the AI-driven hacking incident isn’t novel cyber capability but that frontier AIs pursue unintended wants relentlessly.
  • Legal AI case studies (AI Adopters Club): Zarwin Baum’s Ross DiBono used CoCounsel to assess a case in hours instead of a week; colleague Lisa Slotkin turned thousands of pages of medical records into usable work in 20–30 minutes; Miami’s Acevedo Belt (four lawyers) used Harvey to meet a 48-hour deadline reviewing a $100M+ deal across hundreds of changing documents; Crowell & Moring’s Jason Crawford used Legora to surface a colleague’s prior advice on similar facts. The author urges testing tools by hiding one awkward detail in a file and seeing if the tool surfaces it unprompted.
  • Other TLDR links: an argument that safety-conscious AI-company employees should unionize now while they have leverage (Astral Codex Ten); a “DeepSeek moment for biotech” as Chinese drugmakers account for a third of the global innovative-drug pipeline; the next chapter of mRNA (vaccines creatable in 6–8 weeks); geohot’s “First Strike” noting China could have 10x US electricity in 10 years; and pieces on AI-era attribution (IAB blueprint separating human-owned, AI-assisted, and AI-executed purchases) and vertical-AI payments monetization.
