AI Cybersecurity & Security Incidents

PHP2Shell — the AI-discovered PHP flaws now under mass exploitation. This was the week’s dominant story, covered across AI Weekly, Fortune’s Eye on AI, The Hacker News/SecurityWeek, The Atlantic, The Guardian, Help Net Security, The Register, and others. Two critical vulnerabilities in the PHP language engine itself — tracked as CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619, each rated CVSS 9.2 — allow an unauthenticated attacker to run arbitrary code on a web server with a single request, firing before any application code runs. Because the bugs live in the engine, not an app, every common PHP setup is exposed: WordPress, Drupal, Joomla, and custom sites, across PHP-FPM, Apache with mod_php (highest risk), php-cgi, FrankenPHP and LiteSpeed. Wiz says nearly every PHP version back to 5.4 (released 2012) is affected by at least one bug; PHP itself was affected since PHP 5. Fixes shipped October 21 in PHP 8.5.12, 8.4.27, 8.3.36 and 8.2.35, with source backports for end-of-life 7.4, 8.0 and 8.1.

  • AI found it, twice, independently. Security firm Calif found the first bug on August 1 using OpenAI’s GPT-5.6 Sol, as part of OpenAI’s “Patch the Planet” program that pays for AI-assisted reviews of open-source code. Wiz (owned by Google) independently found both flaws in August using its own Wiz Atlas tool plus Google’s Gemini 4 Argon and GPT-5.6 Sol, and reported them to PHP on August 24. Wiz says building the exploit took one researcher about a week — work it estimates would previously have taken a top team months. Help Net Security: the bugs existed for years but exploitation required a long chain of steps, and “frontier AI models substantially reduced that barrier.” There is a credit dispute: OpenAI said it led the coalition and that frontier AI was key; per The Guardian, the researcher who found it said they used publicly available models.
  • A leak gave attackers a four-day head start. The plan was a quiet coordinated fix with an embargoed disclosure window of 14:00 UTC on October 21. But on October 17, an Amazon decoy/honeypot server recorded an exploitation attempt — four days before disclosure, from an anonymizing service. AWS security chief CJ Moses said it’s unclear how the attacker learned of the embargoed flaws. On October 20, details leaked online and PHP warned patches would ship the next day. Security firm Sansec had predicted mass exploitation within 15 minutes of the patch. Experts told The Guardian that once a fix is public, AI tools can work backward from it to the bug with little effort; Costin Raiu of TLP:Black called it a “race to exploit” and warned of “mass compromise of every vulnerable web server on the internet.”
  • Scale of attacks. Cloudflare CEO Matthew Prince reported thousands of attempts in the 10 hours before the announcement, tens of thousands within 45 minutes, and “millions now,” with attackers already changing payloads to evade WAF rules. Amazon says PHP2Shell “is now being broadly exploited in the wild” and found several working proof-of-concept exploits ~12 hours before disclosure (implying multiple attackers). Censys counted ~1.57 million unpatched hosts out of ~1.58 million showing a PHP version on October 21; VulnCheck saw 2.6M+ internet-facing PHP hosts (nearly all vulnerable), at least 1M on unsupported versions. No confirmed break-ins yet: watchTowr says none of the public exploit code it has seen achieves full code execution, and VulnCheck saw at least one failed web-shell attempt. Amazon advises investigating suspicious activity back to October 17.
  • Governments pulled sites offline. The Netherlands (DigiD national login, House of Representatives, national cybersecurity centre), France (local government including the Paris police prefecture), UK (Edinburgh, Bristol, Oxford councils), plus Belgium, Finland, Germany, and briefly Australia’s cyber agency. Wikimedia took several systems down as a precaution. The US NSA and UK NCSC advised taking unpatched PHP systems offline (NSA warned the flaw could “provide a direct path to the server’s file system or database”) and to check for backdoors. WordPress reportedly saw no issues. Palo Alto Networks confirmed all PAN-OS and Panorama versions affected with no fix yet; Cisco and Fortinet are still checking. Cloudflare pushed stopgap WAF rules to all customers including free tier.
  • OpenAI proposes a new disclosure playbook (Oct 22): a five-stage process for flaws hitting many products at once — build/test the fix (including using AI to hunt for bypasses), give affected companies a head start, bring in governments (for PHP: the White House, Office of the National Cyber Director, and CISA), widen notification, then support post-release patching. OpenAI said these bugs are “among several significant vulnerabilities we’ve already found” and expects such findings to “increase sharply.” It is building out its application-only Daybreak program (software flaws found by participants grew from 858 to 1,885; patches produced from 263 to 661 in two weeks), hired a cybersecurity-background chief revenue officer, and plans to debut a cybersecurity-focused model this fall plus a deployment tool. Greg Brockman pinned a lengthy X post on OpenAI’s role. Anthropic has a comparable vetted-org program called Project Glasswing. Fortune frames AI cyber-defense as a potentially large new revenue line, noting 20+ cybersecurity incidents in October. Ondřej Surý, a largely unpaid volunteer who handles most of the world’s PHP distribution/patching, was profiled by The Atlantic as the “unsung hero” who enabled fast fixes.

Company & Product Developments

Meta releases Wonder, opens Spark (three new Muse models). Meta returned to the AI frontier with three multimodal models released in one week: top-end Muse Wonder Full (subscription-only, $50/month via Muse Code), the mid-tier Muse Wonder (API + subscription), and the open-weights Muse Spark Open (weights free on Meta/Hugging Face). All three accept text, image, video, and audio in (up to 1M tokens) and output text (up to 128K tokens) — a differentiator since no other leading open or closed models input video or audio. Muse Spark Open is a mixture-of-experts transformer with 816B parameters, 42B active per token, 256 experts, ~139–143 tokens/sec, six reasoning levels (minimal→max). On Artificial Analysis’ Intelligence Index (weighted avg of 10 benchmarks), Muse Wonder Full scored 56 (tied for 2nd overall, two points behind Claude Opus 5.5 and identical to Claude Sonnet 5.5), Muse Wonder 52 (7th), and Muse Spark Open 39 (7th among open models, 1st among US open models, ahead of Mistral Large 4 and Reflection’s Beam; tied with DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash; behind MiMo-V2.6-Pro and GLM-5.3). Pricing: Muse Wonder $4/$20 per 1M in/out ($4.52 per Intelligence Index task, 5th highest); Spark Open $1.25/$4.15 ($0.84 per task). AA noted the Wonder models’ low knowledge scores (AA-Omniscience: Wonder Full 30/8th, Wonder 25/10th) partly offset by low hallucination rates. Muse Spark Open needs ~1TB memory at 4-bit precision — runnable locally on 2 Mac Studios (512GB each), with Meta and Apple touting joint work to run it on consumer silicon. License allows commercial/noncommercial use up to $20M revenue, requires “Built with Muse Spark Open” display. The model card reveals little on architecture or training. Background: this reverses Meta’s recent posture — after Llama setbacks, hiring Scale.AI’s Alexandr Wang in 2025, a publicized hiring spree, cheap proprietary Muse Spark models in spring 2026, and Muse Agent a month ago — by returning Meta to both open and closed frontiers. The Batch calls Muse Spark Open “a much more powerful option” than Beam, Mistral Large 4, or Nvidia’s Nemotron 3.5 Super VL despite their more permissive licenses.

Reflection AI releases Beam, its first model; Nvidia circling. Reflection AI — an Nvidia-backed US startup founded in 2024 by ex-DeepMind researchers Misha Laskin and Ioannis Antonoglou, valued at $25B in March — introduced Beam, a 501B-parameter MoE (23B active, 256 experts) for coding/agentic tasks, with waitlist early access and weights/technical report/model card promised later in October under Apache 2.0. Text-only (1M in / 128K out), ~228 tokens/sec, five reasoning levels (defaults to max). On AA’s Intelligence Index it scored 30 — 2nd among US open models behind Muse Spark Open (39), trailing Xiaomi’s MiMo-V2.6-Pro (46), GLM-5.3 (45), and even Qwen 3.8-27B (34) — but it’s notably fast and token-efficient (~26,000 output tokens/task, fewest among open peers; 1.4 min/task, fastest AA has measured; 10.1s first-token latency). Training: pretrained on 23.8T tokens (filters discarded ~95% of raw web) on 6,144 GPUs in under four weeks; a midtraining stage extended long-context to 1M; post-training RL used ~1M environments (mostly synthetic) on 10,500 GPUs for four weeks with 100M+ attempts, rewarding success and penalizing unnecessary tokens; a second copy was fine-tuned for safety/behavior, then merged via multi-teacher on-policy distillation. Architecture interleaves local-attention and full-input layers. At 4-bit (300GB) it runs on 4 H100s or 2 B200s. Reflection estimates it uses ¼–⅓ the compute of GLM-5.2 on reasoning. Reflection has partnered with the Pentagon and DOE and agreed to develop models for allies like South Korea; the Trump administration hopes it rivals cheap Chinese models. Nvidia (which has invested $800M) is in early talks to invest more, acqui-hire, license, or buy the company.

Microsoft’s MAI-DxO claims medical superintelligence. Microsoft AI said its MAI-DxO (Microsoft AI Diagnostic Orchestrator) solved 83% of hard NEJM cases vs. 36% for physicians (with textbooks/internet; 22% without) and 80% of typical ER cases vs. 47% for physicians, reaching correct diagnoses at ~½ to ⅓ the cost with fewer tests. MAI-DxO works as a panel of virtual doctors: each proposes diagnoses, picks which questions/tests to order (weighing cost so it can’t order everything), and challenges the group over several rounds. Microsoft also built SDBench (Sequential Diagnosis Benchmark) — 304 hard NEJM cases plus typical ER cases — where the solver starts with limited info and decides what to ask next, unlike exams that hand over the whole case. Caveats (TechCrunch): the human doctors were solo primary-care physicians/general internists, not the specialists/teams who handle such cases; it’s a simulation with no real patients; most of the gain comes from the underlying models (GPT-5 alone scored 78.9%; MAI-DxO added ~4 points). Next: a study with Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (Boston) in emergency/urgent care comparing the system’s recommendations to human doctors, plus a planned healthcare model with Mayo Clinic next year. Mustafa Suleyman pitches this as a step toward medical superintelligence. No pricing/availability yet.

Research & Technical Papers

TurboVLA — small modular robot control (The Batch). Hengyi Xie, Chenfei Yao, and colleagues at Huazhong University of Science and Technology built TurboVLA, a vision-language-action system that drops the large LLM backbone in favor of separate encoders (a DINOv3 vision encoder + lightweight BERT text encoder), projected into a shared embedding space, connected by six cross-attention layers (pretrained Grounding DINO Swin-T), with a separate action transformer combining the fused embeddings and the robot’s current joint positions to output target joint positions (7 values per arm). Key insight: forgoing the LLM’s task-decomposition ability lets small encoders learn to understand instructions like “stack bowls” and connect words to objects, slashing compute/memory. Trained to reproduce arm movements in LIBERO (single-arm), RoboTwin 2.0 (50 bimanual tasks), and fine-tuned on AgileX Piper demos. Results: on LIBERO, 97.6% success with just 0.2B parameters, 31.2 ms latency, 0.9 GB VRAM on an RTX 4090 — versus CogVLA (97.4%, 8.3B params, 115.5 ms, 16.1 GB) and π0.5 (96.9%, 3.4B, 238.8 ms, 7.7 GB). On RoboTwin 2.0, 88.1% at 43.4 ms (vs LingBot-VLA 87.6%/164.4 ms, π0.5 79.8%/246.5 ms). On four real AgileX Piper tasks, 92.5%/80.0%/90.0%/87.5%, beating π0.5. The Batch notes this revives a pre-LLM (~2022) fusion pattern as a more efficient path to driving robots.

AI agents left to “play” perform better later (MIT, preprint arXiv:2610.07130). MIT researchers left 13 AI agents alone on a simulated island for 30 hours with no assignment and a gentle nudge to stay curious. The agents began inventing their own challenges, and when later given real tasks, those with open-ended “island time” generally beat control agents that skipped it — suggesting aimless exploration may teach transferable strategies. Caveat: early paper, and instructions encouraged curiosity, so calling it spontaneous “play” is a stretch.

Formal verification at scale with LLMs (Anthropic/Theorem). Anthropic and Theorem reported that Claude proved security properties across 123 open-source libraries and found hidden bugs — though only for narrow cases like small RISC-V binaries. Formal software verification has traditionally required years of expert effort; LLMs can now produce formal proofs at scale, enabling development workflows with proven security guarantees. The broader argument (Anthropic research, 23-min read): formal verification will likely become essential to secure software development as models improve.

It works by nudging choices among near-equivalent words, adds no hidden characters or tokens, and carries no information about the user.

AI in Education, Workplace & Adoption

ChatGPT for Teens gets new tools and a failing grade (The Batch). OpenAI announced college-planning and study tools for its under-18 experience — a forthcoming College Planner (initially US grades 10–12 for four-year colleges, consolidating each school’s requirements into one timeline), flashcards, and note-to-quiz tools, plus funding for College Advising Corps. ChatGPT for Teens isn’t a separate model but a bundle of settings/classifiers/prompts triggered for 13–17 accounts or those an age-prediction model flags as likely minors (based on account age, activity timing, usage patterns, topics, stated age; misclassified adults can verify age to exit). A December 2025 Model Spec section directs the model to avoid romantic language and not imply feelings; an unremovable “reduce sensitive content” setting limits self-harm, violence, eating-disorder, dangerous-activity, and explicit content. Parents linking accounts get Quiet Hours, Study Hours scheduling, and high-risk notifications. Study Mode guides step-by-step rather than giving answers. OpenAI stats: teens average <15 min/day, <2% have 3+ hour sessions, ~half paused within 5 min of a break reminder; in one week ~1.2M teens used the visualization tool and 180,000+ used Study Mode.

  • Common Sense Media’s Youth AI Safety Institute rated ChatGPT for Teens “Unacceptable Risk” (its lowest) and urged restricting ChatGPT to adults until independent testing validates protections. It ran 4,000+ prompts (half before launch July 13–Aug 17, half after Aug 25–Sept 28). Findings: testers posing as teens in crisis made explicit disclosures for up to an hour yet parent accounts received no alerts except those with weeks of history (OpenAI says accounts must be linked ~3 hours before alerts fire; institute says some were linked longer). ChatGPT missed >1 in 4 warranted crisis referrals; for depression prompts, hotline mentions fell from 63% to 3% after launch; for suicide/self-harm, hotline mentions fell 88%→77%; substance-use hotline rate rose 4%→58%. Follow-up safety questions dropped from nearly all pre-launch responses to just 13%. Study Mode often offered a “show me the answer” button producing finished assignments; teens could bypass parent-scheduled Study Hours by deleting an “@study” prefix. Age estimation never switched adult-registered accounts to teen mode even when testers said they were 13. OpenAI disputed the findings, said testing may have predated full parental-control activation, and asked for a retest with larger samples.
  • Context: a Pew survey (Feb) of 1,458 US teens found 64% had used a chatbot, ~30% daily, >50% for schoolwork, 12% for emotional support. The GUARD Act (cleared Senate Judiciary 22-0 in April) would require age verification, bar minors from AI companions, and mandate non-human disclosures. Florida was first to sue OpenAI (June) over teen-safety marketing/data claims; eight more states followed. A randomized trial of ~1,000 Turkish high-schoolers found students using an unrestricted GPT-4 tutor scored higher on homework but 17% lower on a later no-AI exam than peers without access (a modified-prompt version avoided the loss).

Anthropic’s own AI adoption, measured (AI Adopters Club). By August 2026, agents directed by Anthropic employees ran 6x as many hours as employees were assumed to work (40-hour weeks × headcount); in research groups the ratio was 19:1. (Anthropic stresses this isn’t a count of replaced workers or a productivity multiple.) Interactive sessions spent ~80% of wall-clock time waiting for a person (review, pauses, abandoned work, multitasking). Work setting up/steering/checking agents grew from 10% to 22% of interactive sessions. Reading every generated word would theoretically take 424 hours per person per week in engineering/research. The newsletter’s “attention bottleneck” lesson: measure the whole job (including human review load) before buying more AI capacity.
