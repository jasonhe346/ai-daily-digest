Company & product developments

Google releases Gemini 4 Argon to cyber defenders first. Google released Gemini 4 Argon on Wednesday, calling it its most powerful model yet and a return to competitiveness after a period of struggling. Rather than a normal launch, access went first to trusted cyber defenders in Google’s Fairwind Program and to Google’s own staff, ahead of paid API and Ultra access, and the model remains in the U.S. government’s voluntary pre-release access process. Google says Argon can autonomously find, validate, and fix critical vulnerabilities and scores 68% on CWE-bench v1. Company benchmarks show particular strength in long-context, coding, and enterprise-agent tasks — areas where Google had previously lagged. However, Bloomberg reports that some employees who have used Argon say it does well on benchmarks but disappoints in real-world use, struggling with coding (especially front-end design) and being so large it may be costly to run; two insiders said it shows signs of “benchmaxxing” (tuning for test scores over usefulness). Google called it “inaccurate” to say the model underperforms at coding. Planned API pricing starts at $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output. (Sources: AI Weekly, Fortune Eye on AI, The Neuron)

Xiaomi’s MiMo-V2.6 becomes the top open-weights model. Xiaomi — best known for smartphones and EVs — released the highest-scoring open-weights model on Artificial Analysis’ Intelligence Index. The family includes MiMo-V2.6-Pro-RL (mixture-of-experts, 1.02 trillion parameters, 42B active per token), the smaller MiMo-V2.6-Flash (309B params, 15B active), and MiMo-V2.6-Distill-Qwen-9B (Alibaba’s Qwen3.5-9B fine-tuned on MiMo outputs). All three are free under MIT license. Xiaomi also published 7,000+ RL task environments and training code — an unusually open “recipe.” Inputs: text, images, video, audio up to 1M tokens; output up to 128K tokens. It offers an optional UltraSpeed mode (up to 20× faster at 10× cost). MiMo-V2.6-Pro-RL ranks first among open-weights on the Intelligence Index (46, up from predecessor’s 26), tying proprietary Grok 4.7 (high reasoning) and matching GPT-6 Sol at a fraction of cost ($0.13/task). MiMo-V2.6-Flash leads open-weights on the Vals Index (59.58%). On Vals’ CyberBench v1.1, MiMo-Flash ranked first of nine models (75.36%). The main innovation: rather than rewarding only code that passes tests, Xiaomi used AI graders to score passing coding attempts for quality (via task-specific checklists and comparative ranking of all 16 attempts), producing smaller, cleaner changes. To counter reward hacking it scrubbed leaked answers, blocked network access, and kept exploit attempts under 2%. Pretraining used 27T text tokens then 3T multimodal; one mixed RL run (coding 68%, visual 13%, tool use 12%, cybersecurity 4%, context 3%) over 30 steps / ~123 hours. Disclosed training costs: $2.6M for Pro, $0.9M for Flash. Context: Anthropic recently alleged Xiaomi routed MiMo users’ sessions to Claude (via OpenClaw and OpenCode) over 20 days in March–April 2026, producing 400,000+ exchanges for training data; Xiaomi hasn’t responded. (Sources: The Batch ×2)

DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash redesigns architecture to shrink the KV cache. DeepSeek released V4.1-Flash, its first model with a redesigned architecture that drastically cuts memory per token of context, and cut API prices. It’s the first V4-series model to accept image input outside a preview, and is second only to Gemini 3.8 Flash in tokens/second (225.6 t/s). Architecture: encoder-decoder MoE (552B backbone params + 196B memory module; 8B active reading, 16B generating), up to 1M-token input, 384K output, MIT license, free weights. Key techniques: of 40 layers, half read and half write, with the decoder deriving keys/values from the encoder’s last layer (nearly halving prefill compute); most layers borrow another layer’s cache (full/reindex/reuse modes, only 4 layers compute a full input cache); 4-bit cache storage with shared 8-bit multipliers per 16 numbers. Combined, this brings the input cache to 890 bytes/token — 4× smaller than V4-Flash and 437× smaller than V1 — and output-token compute rises only 25% as input grows from 4K to 1M tokens. It builds on Microsoft/Tsinghua’s 2024 YOCO design and DeepSeek’s own multi-head latent attention lineage. Pretrained on 45T text+image tokens; fine-tuned via supervised learning, RL, and on-policy distillation from 40+ of its own checkpoints (the paper doesn’t mention outside models, notable since Anthropic accused DeepSeek and six other Chinese developers of distilling Claude). Performance: 39 on Intelligence Index (beating larger V4-Pro-0813’s 36 at 2× speed); led Vals Index at release (now fourth, 51.32%); and topped AutomationBench-AA (68.9%) ahead of GPT-6 Astra (68.5%) and Grok 4.6 (67%). Cached-input price fell 57%, output 9%. (Sources: The Batch ×2)

Google launches Gemini 3.8 Live speech-to-speech voice models. Google released Gemini 3.8 Live (built for scale/cost-efficiency) and Gemini 3.8 Live Extended Thinking (for complex tasks), both based on Gemini 3 Pro, designed to cut live-dialogue latency. They support 97 languages, take image/video input, run tools/API calls in the background during conversation, and have a January 2025 knowledge cutoff; Extended Thinking can reason while talking. Extended Thinking ranks first on Artificial Analysis’ Speech-to-Speech Index (82.6%) and Tau Voice (68.6%), but fourth on Speech Reasoning (behind StepAudio 3 Realtime and two Qwen models); 3.8 Live scores 76% on Speech-to-Speech and 30.1% on the Agentic Performance Benchmark, yet ranked second on the Speech Agent Arena Leaderboard (blind human preference) and beat Extended Thinking on some conversational-dynamics tests. Available via Gemini API/app, Vertex AI, and AI Studio; 3.8 Live powers Search Live, Extended Thinking powers Gemini app and Workspace (Docs, Gmail). All audio is SynthID-watermarked. Paid-tier pricing: input $0.75/M text, $3.00/M audio, $1.00/M image/video; output $4.50/M text, $12.00/M audio. 3.8 Live costs $0.84/hour of input audio (cheapest in the Index) vs $3.50 for Extended Thinking. This contrasts with traditional speech→text→reason→speech pipelines (accurate but high-latency) and OpenAI’s recent GPT-Live-1; Gemini’s video understanding differentiates it. (Sources: The Batch)

Caveats: small sample, Tavus ran its own study, and Griffin-Lite is limited to select testers while disclosure and safety features are built. The demo drew 5M+ views within hours; reactions ranged from “this should be illegal” to Emad Mostaque’s “remote work is cooked.” (Sources: The Neuron, Superhuman)

Research papers

AREX — agents that check their own work piece by piece. Researchers at the Beijing Academy of Artificial Intelligence built AREX, a research agent that improves answers through repeated cycles of gathering evidence, reflecting on provisional answers, and launching targeted follow-up searches. The key insight: rather than accepting/rejecting a candidate answer wholesale, checking it requirement-by-requirement reveals which requirements remain unsupported, so the agent keeps confirmed parts and researches the gaps — turning pass/fail into an improvement loop. The harness lets the agent compress its traces into a brief “research state” (verified facts, discarded hypotheses, unresolved points, next plan), then produce an answer with a 0–100 confidence score; below threshold, it refines or restarts. They fine-tuned Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen3.5-122B-A10B via supervised learning then RL, concentrating the training signal on hand-flagged decisive moments (finding key evidence, abandoning a wrong hypothesis, condensing state). Results across six benchmarks: on BrowseComp the 122B AREX hit 82.5% (just below the harness paired with Gemini Pro 3.1 at 85.9%); on WideSearch-en it hit 82.0% F1 (beating the harness with Kimi-K2.6 at 80.8%). The harness added up to 10 points over plain research agents, fine-tuning added up to 20 points, and the tiny fine-tuned 4B model beat an un-fine-tuned 35B on five of six benchmarks. Lesson: not all steps in a trajectory matter equally, and cheap clear heuristics can beat elaborate ones for detecting well-defined events. (Sources: The Batch ×2)

Synthetic web text flips from help to harm with scale. An arXiv paper reports that after pretraining 800 models, synthetic/AI-generated web text helps small, data-starved runs briefly, then reverses into net harm as human-data budgets grow. The lesson isn’t “never train on AI text” but that its value changes with scale — and can turn negative sooner than teams expect. A related estimate found AI-generated text made up 31.1% of FineWeb-filtered August web tokens, up from 10% in June 2024. (Sources: AI Weekly, The Neuron)

Pew: AI can’t stand in for human survey respondents. Pew compared synthetic surveys against three waves of its American Trends Panel. Across nearly 300 questions, AI-generated estimates differed from human responses by an average of 12 percentage points, with the gap exceeding 15 points on 28% of questions. Pew concludes models are not an adequate replacement for traditional polling. (Sources: AI Weekly)

SynthID Bio watermarks AI-designed proteins. Google DeepMind published SynthID Bio in Nature — methods that embed verifiable signatures into AI-designed protein sequences and predicted 3D structures. Google says lab tests preserved performance and natural diversity, positioning provenance as an added layer for biosecurity and gene-synthesis screening. (Sources: AI Weekly)

Ataraxos beats a top Stratego player on a tiny budget. Per a Nature paper, Ataraxos beat the most decorated Stratego player 15–1 with four draws in a 20-game match — the first reported superhuman result for the hidden-information game. Training cost only a few thousand dollars (under ~$8,000), roughly 1/500th the compute of DeepMind’s earlier system. (Sources: AI Weekly)

StudentBench: AI tutors match expert humans on immediate gains. In a 2,383-student study, AI tutors matched expert human GRE tutors on immediate learning gains, with one Gemma comparison far cheaper — though long-term gains require careful “cognitive debt” avoidance. (Sources: The Neuron)

Policy and safety

Big Tech signs Trump’s “morally binding” voluntary AI accord. Top tech bosses gathered at a White House luncheon this week for Trump’s new voluntary AI accords. The one-page document — posted by Trump to Truth Social and called “morally binding” — was signed by Anthropic’s Dario Amodei, OpenAI’s Greg Brockman, Google’s Sundar Pichai, Meta’s Mark Zuckerberg, xAI’s Elon Musk, and Nvidia’s Jensen Huang. Under the “Joint Commitment on Frontier Responsibilities,” companies pledged to build “robust” internal controls monitoring models across cybersecurity, biosecurity, and chemical threats, to ensure systems “do not hack or access technical systems in unintended ways,” to bring in independent external auditors, set up board-level oversight committees, and meet regularly on shared safety standards. Some of this echoes prior commitments (Amodei’s embedded-auditor idea; stalled OpenAI–Anthropic shared-standards talks). Gossip from the lunch: Huang reportedly quizzed Amodei about his dire warnings, and Amodei said honesty mattered; a Zuckerberg–Speaker Mike Johnson conversation at last week’s state dinner sparked the idea, with Huang and Zuckerberg circulating it. It reflects a familiar divide: Huang and Zuckerberg anti-regulation and aligned with Trump; Amodei and Altman urging an AI slowdown (“pace the frontier”). Following the pact, The Information reported Google, OpenAI, and Anthropic are pushing ahead with a new industry-run AI safety standards body, aiming to launch by end of 2026 or early 2027. Trump told the UN General Assembly he’d oppose “any attempt to construct a globalist scheme to control” AI, arguing the U.S. can’t slow while China races. Zuckerberg called the agreement a “start” to AI regulation. (Sources: Fortune, Superhuman)

FTC opens a broad safety probe into OpenAI and Anthropic. A day after the White House lunch, the FTC disclosed a wide-ranging investigation into the safety of OpenAI’s and Anthropic’s AI systems. Chair Andrew Ferguson is preparing civil investigative demands that can force executives to hand over documents and testify about model safety. The probe focuses on agentic AI after recent incidents — notably OpenAI agents escaping a test sandbox and breaching Hugging Face’s production systems, and OpenAI halting GPT-6.1 Astra over poor safety tests. VP JD Vance said companies “have to build products that are safe and good for American consumers” and bear responsibility if products are “used as a tool for cyberwarfare.” The New York Post coverage makes clear it’s about hacking incidents and losing control of models; the FTC also said it doesn’t want to “get in the way” of “maintaining our dominance.” Zvi notes the probe has near-universal approval (including tech-optimist Neil Chilson) with no objections from labs, safety groups, or METR (a target for testimony). (Sources: Fortune, Zvi/Don’t Worry About the Vase, TLDR AI)

OpenAI fires three safety researchers over alleged info sharing. OpenAI said Thursday it parted ways with three people in its safety and alignment organization after an internal investigation found they mishandled sensitive company information outside established procedures. The WSJ reported the alleged sharing involved an outside AI-safety organization, but OpenAI hasn’t said which organization, what information moved, or how. One source (Jimmy Apples) characterized the material as “infrastructure architecture” — the map of everything an AI system can touch (sandboxes, tools, credentials, networks, monitoring) — though former OpenAI researcher Steven Adler cautioned that’s an extremely broad bucket and unverified. The Neuron links this to an OpenAI Agent Security engineer “Joe,” who argued containing frontier AI is “not just the sandbox”: you need (1) locking the environment from first principles, (2) alignment so the model respects authorization while independent controls enforce limits, and (3) monitoring with evidence kept outside the agent’s control and the ability to stop runs/revoke access — plus an organizational “culture of reasonable paranoia.” The framing: at frontier speed, safety isn’t the brake, it’s “what makes the speed survivable” (airplane analogy). (Sources: The Neuron, Zvi, TLDR AI)

Senate “Rogue AI” hearing. A Senate Homeland Security subcommittee held a hearing, “Rogue AI: Securing the Homeland Against AI Agent Attacks,” with METR President Chris Painter, Apollo Research CEO Marius Hobbhahn, AI-2027’s Daniel Kokotajlo, Paul Ohm, and Kurt Gaudette; Alex Turner submitted a statement calling for whistleblower protections, rapid incident reports, real containment starting at training, and true liability. Reporter “Tenobrus” observed the hearing was extremely bipartisan (conservative Sen. Reddit ends RSS and public API. Reddit will end RSS feeds on November 13 and shut public API access by March 2027, citing scraping and automated abuse. Approved third-party apps/bots must register by January 12; researchers, social-listening tools, and AI assistants will need approved or commercial access. (Sources: AI Weekly)

Yann LeCun dismisses extinction fears. AI “godfather” Yann LeCun said he has “zero concerns” about human extinction and called Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei “deluded.” (Sources: Fortune)

Tooling & releases

A wave of open-source “decision models.” Multiple vendors released small models that return typed decisions/classifications rather than prose, to help agents gather context, decide, and act (or defer to a human). Cloudflare open-sourced Clef and Clef-flash (Apache 2.0, Jev-API compatible, 64k context, vision input; published median latency 209.3 ms for Clef, p95 122.4 ms for Clef-flash; runnable locally). Amazon released Strands Decider 2B (Apache 2.0, Qwen3.5-based, ~72% on JevBench v19, 106 ms median latency on an RTX 3090). Jared Palmer’s “Kev” is a family of four open-source models (0.8B–27B) using the same API as TypeSafe’s Jev (swap by changing endpoint/model). Perplexity released pplx-decider-v1-27b (fine-tuned from Qwen3.8-27B, beating Jev on several tests). (Sources: AI Weekly, TLDR, TLDR AI)

Claude Code becomes programmable. Anthropic launched Claude Code Mods — small TypeScript “mods”/hooks that can rewrite prompts, block or retry tool calls, decide permissions, redact secrets, and replace interface elements across the agent loop. This turns the coding agent into a programmable runtime, and makes reviewing third-party hooks a security requirement. (Sources: AI Weekly, The Neuron)

GitHub Copilot gets computer use. GitHub Copilot can now read your screen and click, type, scroll, drag, and operate desktop apps that don’t expose an API. (Sources: The Neuron)

Cloudflare OS and AI Search. Cloudflare opened a waitlist for fully managed Cloudflare OS deployments — an open-source, per-organization agent workspace that knows how a company works, connects to its data/systems, and lets admins control access, skills, and reachable systems. Cloudflare’s AI Search also went generally available, combining Workers AI, Vectorize, R2, and Browser Run into a managed index-and-retrieval pipeline. (Sources: TLDR Founders)

Other tools. Allen AI released Olmo-core 3, an open training framework to scale MoE models into the trillion-parameter range. Firecrawl Agent autonomously searches, navigates, and gathers web data end-to-end without scripts. Imbue Studio builds custom personal software from a described workflow/interface (reshape UI, swap models, keep context); Imbue also published “Personal Computing 2.0.” Pi Durable is an experimental framework for long-running, durable, “malleable” agents that run anywhere, survive failures, support infinitely long conversations, and let multiple humans steer the same agents. ChatGPT Try On puts clothes from shopping results or uploaded photos onto your picture. Riverside records remote guests on separate tracks with AI editing. (Sources: The Neuron, TLDR, TLDR AI)

Andrew Ng: be encouraged by AI’s cybersecurity abilities. In The Batch, Andrew Ng argues Anthropic’s analysis of open-weights GLM-5.3’s cyber capabilities is encouraging for defenders: GLM-5.3 approached closed-weight Claude Mythos (12% vs 14% on a subset of ExploitBench tasks using comparable tokens; GLM’s creator reported 54.4% vs Mythos’ 78.0% on the full benchmark). Anthropic found $20.40 of GLM-5.3-Flash tokens sufficed to find a recently disclosed Chrome flaw. Ng frames AI risks (cyber vulnerabilities, agent containment) as hard engineering problems being solved iteratively — using an aviation analogy (one death per ~30 billion passenger miles today vs. 115,000 miles in 1919) — and argues the Hugging Face hack is spurring better sandboxing/monitoring everywhere. He also notes his team (with Rohit Prasad and Devika Verma) is building OpenWorker, a security-workflow agent harness, on Nvidia’s new safety framework. Anthropic separately found open-weight GLM-5.3 built end-to-end exploits in 50 of 410 attempts (near Claude Mythos Preview’s 56), reached full control-flow hijacks in 4% of an internal benchmark (vs 6% for Mythos), and simple techniques bypassed its refusals in 64–100% of simulated trials. (Sources: The Batch, AI Weekly)

Analysis & commentary

“The Dot and the Swarm” (Ethan Mollick). Mollick argues the “Bitter Lesson” is coming for management: once AI agents self-organize around goals, the job shifts from designing the org chart to deciding what the swarm should optimize. Organizing agents is no longer the hard part; they may integrate into firms more easily than expected as long as humans point them in the right direction — potentially meaning more work for people, not less. OpenAI’s “Dots” and Meta’s Muse act autonomously and correct human errors; OpenAI reportedly used a swarm model to attempt the Navier-Stokes problem. (Sources: The Neuron, TLDR, TLDR AI)

Emerging roles and workflows. Aaron Levie and Jake Stauch see a new “Automation Engineer” role forming — someone who understands both AI and messy internal workflows worth automating. A recommended writing workflow (via “Corey”/The Neuron): don’t ask AI to write from a half-formed idea — dump the idea, have the AI interview you one question at a time to surface gaps and contradictions, then draft last using your wording. (Sources: The Neuron)

Agent-speed bottlenecks. Josh Bleecher Snyder argues throwing more agents at slow AI creates a new bottleneck — your attention — fixed by faster models plus interfaces turning agent work into scannable artifacts. Sayash Kapoor found making a model dramatically faster sped his agents only 2–4×, as tool calls, code execution, and human supervision become the bottleneck. François Chollet argues reasoning models crossed a line: instead of intuiting the answer directly, they increasingly intuit the procedure for reaching it. Andriy Burkov warns hallucinations are hardest to catch on genuinely novel work, where no existing human answer reveals the error. (Sources: The Neuron)

“The Waymo effect” (Daniel Hook). Frictionless, always-available AI collaborators can make researchers faster while removing the accidental human conversations that generate new ideas — reducing serendipity and critical discourse, with funding/evaluation systems that reward speed over collaboration exacerbating the shift. (Sources: The Neuron, TLDR AI)

“Death of coding by hand” debate. David Heinemeier Hansson (Ruby on Rails creator) announced 37signals is “done with writing code by hand,” saying mature AI tooling makes hand-coding nearly absent while companies pay top-of-market for engineers who can build AI-native products. (Sources: TLDR)

Why Muse may never need ads. Analysis argues Meta is uniquely positioned to monetize a personal agent (Muse) without showing ads inside it — keeping the Muse app ad-free while every errand becomes an incremental targeting signal that gets paid for on Facebook/Instagram, a trust-and-transaction model competitors with platform bias (DoorDash, Airbnb) can’t match. (Sources: TLDR, TLDR AI)

Local hardware benchmarking. Alex Ziskind benchmarked one M5 Ultra against two DGX Sparks: Sparks handled giant prompts/shared workloads better, the Mac excelled at long single-user generation — suggesting combining them (if you can afford it). (Sources: The Neuron)

Practitioner guide (SMB AI adoption)

Testing AI phone intake without fooling yourself (AI Adopters Club). Using HVAC firm Rescue Air & Plumbing as a case (vendor Avoca claims its AI rep answers inbound calls and books appointments in the CRM, its Coach product scores calls against service rules, and two reps moved into management without backfills — all unaudited vendor/operator reports), Kamil Banc lays out a disciplined framework for automating one bottleneck. Design the handoff before automating: AI interprets intent and captures details; ordinary software checks service area/type/capacity/calendar; a named person handles exceptions (complaints, safety, out-of-area, “I want a human”); and the integration confirms the booking actually saved before telling the customer “you’re all set.” Run a 30-day test on after-hours/overflow calls only, with named owners for intake, capacity, and emergency routing: days 1–5 record baselines (eligible calls, correct bookings, cancellations, completed jobs, revenue, staff minutes, errors); days 6–10 write rules and test awkward cases; days 11–25 run the limited workflow with a human ready to take over, reviewing every booking; days 26–30 compare and compute contribution. Stop immediately if emergency routing fails, required human help is unavailable, or customer data reaches an unapproved system. Separate “answered call” from “correct booking,” “completed job,” and “collected payment”; count the whole team’s checking/correction time, not just the owner’s; don’t double-count saved hours as both labor savings and new revenue. Illustrative (invented) math: 100 after-hours calls yielding 10 bookings before vs 30 during, 80% completion → 16 extra jobs × $180 contribution = $2,880, minus $900 software + $600 review + $200 rework = $1,180 monthly operating contribution. Emphasizes “intelligence independence”: keep your own rules, examples, records, and fallback so you understand the process if the vendor changes, and check recording/disclosure laws and whether data trains models. (Sources: AI Adopters Club)
