Company & product developments

OpenAI launches GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol. OpenAI rolled out GPT-6.2 Astra and GPT-6.2 Sol on Tuesday across ChatGPT Work, Codex and the API, available to Plus, Pro, Enterprise and Edu users. Astra is the new frontier model aimed at the hardest coding, research, document and spreadsheet projects, priced at $8/$40 per million input/output tokens — 20% below GPT-6 Astra — while Sol is an efficient everyday model holding GPT-6.1 Sol’s $2/$10 rates. OpenAI says Astra sets new highs on work and science evals at under a third of GPT-6 Astra’s cost per task, topping its previous model by 5.2 points on a multistep professional test; Sol nearly matches Astra on Cognition’s FrontierCode 2.0 at roughly one-fifth the cost. Both ship with roughly 1M-token context windows and produce better results with fewer output tokens, so users get more done within the same usage limits; Pro tiers unlock an “Ultrafast” decode mode up to 4.5x standard speed. GPT-6.2 ranks #2 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index. However, early feedback has been rough: VentureBeat called the reception “essentially unusable” from AI power users, who say Astra is hard to steer and sometimes stops short; Reddit users flagged repetitive creative ideas; and Wharton’s Ethan Mollick found it uneven despite impressive results on ambitious projects. OpenAI also published a prompt-injection robustness evaluation for the models, which measures resistance to (not immunity from) indirect injection attacks. (OpenAI)

Microsoft unveils seven MAI models and “Copilot as the OS for work” at Ignite 2026. Microsoft AI released seven new specialized models built for strong performance at low prices, spanning coding, cybersecurity, image editing, audio/real-time voice, and decision-making. Its MAI Code 2 model, now in GitHub Copilot, reportedly reaches 98% of GPT-6.1 Sol’s coding quality at about 40% of the cost. Microsoft-Decision-1.1 became generally available for fast, low-cost calibrated decisions under uncertainty across real-world software use cases. CEO Satya Nadella announced the general availability of Word, Excel and PowerPoint running inside Copilot, framing Copilot as “the new OS for work.” Microsoft also unveiled Copilot Workspaces (a shared space where people and agents work together) and Agent 365 Business Analytics (to help companies measure how AI spending translates to business value), plus new agent experiences. (Microsoft)

Google expands Gemini Tasks to free users. Google is extending Tasks — a Gemini mode for handling recurring, multi-step everyday jobs — beyond paid plans to free users in six countries. Instead of chatting back and forth, you toggle Gemini’s home screen from Chat to Task and describe what you need; Gemini then assembles itineraries, runs recurring background research, and can make phone calls, book appointments and complete purchases within a spending limit (more advanced actions have their own rollout and permissions). It’s powered by Gemini 4 Hydrogen. Google VP Josh Woodward said the tool helped him sort medical bills, track insurance renewals, and figure out why his electric bill was spiking. (Google)

OpenAI ChatGPT Wallet and ChatGPT for Sales. OpenAI is rolling out ChatGPT Wallet, letting ChatGPT find products, compare options and complete checkout inside the conversation with built-in Visa, Mastercard, Chase and American Express integration. By design it is cautious: for each purchase you review items, total cost and payment card, then approve with Face ID or fingerprint, and the card network issues a token so the agent never touches your real details. Fully delegated buying with spending limits remains a longer-term goal. The launch arrived the same week an AI shopping agent added four cartons of 18 eggs (72 total) nobody requested to a user’s grocery cart — equity analyst Matt Smith shared the screenshot on X, calling it his first real-world consequence of an AI hallucination; when asked why, the agent admitted it had no reason. ChatGPT Wallet joins Meta’s Muse (launched September), Instinct, and new entrant Daydream (a fashion stylist that builds outfits, offers virtual try-ons and completes purchases). Separately, OpenAI introduced ChatGPT for Sales, a plugin offering ready-to-use workflows that pull from tools sales teams already use to prepare for calls, tailor proposals and follow up; availability varies by plan, region and workplace settings. Amazon, meanwhile, is reportedly building a “World Product Graph” — a database of every product for sale on Earth (including items Amazon doesn’t carry) — to steer outside AI agents toward Amazon; a Morgan Stanley report cited by Reuters notes only 16% of shoppers start product research on Amazon versus 57% on Google. (OpenAI Wallet)

Li joins AMD as executive vice president and chief scientist reporting to CEO Lisa Su, with co-founders Justin Johnson and Ben Mildenhall continuing to lead the team. (AMD)

OpenAI revealed the first neocloud associated with the AMD deal, TensorWave, which will provide 457 megawatts across multiple US sites — just 7.6% of the deal’s total but TensorWave’s largest-ever deployment, requiring it to roughly triple staff. Chips, infrastructure & energy

Nvidia Vera Rubin sweeps MLPerf Training v6.1. Nvidia’s new Vera Rubin NVL72 platform swept every benchmark in MLPerf Training v6.1 at debut, delivering up to 2x the per-GPU performance of the GB300 NVL72 and scaling from 256 to 1,024 GPUs at ~97% efficiency on DeepSeek-V3 (cutting training time from 16 to ~4 minutes). On a provisional Kimi K3 agentic inference test, Vera Rubin NVL72 posted more than 10x the throughput per watt of GB300 at the same response speed. CoreWeave reported first multi-rack Vera Rubin cloud results in the same round. The announcements came during the Supercomputing 2026 conference. (Nvidia)

Anthropic’s ~$900B compute commitments through 2045. Anthropic’s IPO filings show $900.9 billion in infrastructure commitments through 2045, including $7.2 billion a year for five years on the first 1-GW tranche of Google TPUs across four sites. About $451.6 billion is expected to come due before the end of 2030. A new “Anthropic Direct” plan lets the company lease its own data centers rather than relying only on AWS, Google and Azure. Anthropic relies on four main chip providers — Google TPUs, AMD GPUs, Amazon Trainium, and Nvidia GPUs — and has built a “proprietary orchestration layer” that optimizes which workloads run on which chips to reduce costs, a competitive advantage over Nvidia-dependent OpenAI. Anthropic told IPO investors that Claude Mythos Preview ported nearly all the kernels needed to run one Anthropic model on AMD MI355X GPUs in about three days with “basically no human intervention” — work that chief compute officer Tom Brown said historically took dozens of engineers six to twelve months. CEO Dario Amodei called computing power the company’s “deepest business dilemma”: buy too much and costs outrun revenue, buy too little and customers leave. Anthropic also faces an electricity problem, exploring natural gas and posting a job for its first “Gas Supply and Transport Lead” (paying up to $405,000). (FT)

Harvard study: US hyperscale data centers emit as much CO2 as Denmark. A Harvard-led study in Nature Sustainability estimates 403 US hyperscale data centers consumed 68 to 99 terawatt-hours from May 2024 to April 2025 and emitted roughly 25.7 million metric tons of CO2 — comparable to Denmark or Bolivia. That is about 2% of US electricity, more than New York City and Los Angeles combined.

Google’s geothermal and Nvidia’s energy footprint. Houston-based geothermal startup Quaise Energy signed a deal with Google valued at more than $500 million for 50 megawatts of power; Quaise uses technology to vaporize rock and drill at superhot depths, building a site in central Oregon whose electricity will support Google’s existing data center operations there. (WSJ)

Cerebras launches disaggregated inference. Cerebras’ new disaggregated inference service lets GPUs handle prompt processing while Cerebras systems handle output generation, delivering five times more tokens per megawatt at the same ultrafast speeds. It’s in early access with access expanding. (Cerebras)

Security & cyber threats

Google flags “exploit_agent” as the first autonomous AI attack in the wild. Google’s Threat Intelligence Group identified a threat actor using an LLM framework called exploit_agent — configured with Russian-language tooling — that could identify vulnerabilities and automatically develop exploits without human intervention. Google says it’s the first such discovery and that wider adoption of agentic tools by bad actors has “crossed a significant threshold,” describing a “critical inflection point.” Its November 16 report states researchers have now identified AI frameworks that can find software flaws, develop exploits, and execute attacks against real targets without a human in the loop. (Google)

FBI warns about ARTEX autonomous-hacking framework. The FBI (TLP:CLEAR November 2026 issue) warned that hackers are using ARTEX, an open-source AI-enhanced penetration-testing platform, to run autonomous cyberattacks across the US and globally. Attackers describe a goal in plain English (“get into their email”) and specialized AI agents plan and execute with minimal human interaction; ARTEX plugs into hosted models from Anthropic and OpenAI plus open-source and non-US models (the FBI found Chinese models dominate attacker deployments, with DeepSeek leading due to low cost and lack of observable guardrails). It scans for weaknesses, exploits vulnerabilities, abuses exposed/stolen passwords, inventories which machines lack endpoint protection, maps trust between organizations, compromises a weak vendor, then pivots upstream. Targets include financial services, government, tech, defense, telecom and law firms; some intrusions go from initial targeting to privileged access in under three hours. Documented campaigns: a South Korean financial-institution attack (no MFA) that stole data on ~67,000 people; attempted access to the Microsoft 365 environments of 2,250+ downstream customers via IT company ConnectWise; and exploitation at Barracuda of a cloud access key given to a third-party vendor but never revoked. The developer removed the repository in October, but forks, mirrors and a commercial “ARTEX PRO” keep it circulating; as of early November researchers found 1,600+ ARTEX consoles exposed to the public internet. Recommended defenses: MFA, system updates, retiring stale credentials, and least-privilege access.

Widespread outages hit banks, air travel, the NSA and Apple. Over roughly four days, outages disrupted Microsoft services, the FAA, JPMorgan Chase transactions, and Apple products (Apple Pay, iCloud, App Store); the NSA’s internal “NSA Net” reportedly went down. Hackers also hijacked Optimizely Web, a popular website testing tool, to serve malware to visitors across 178 organizations including Zoom, CNN, the BBC, Salesforce and Equifax. Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton dismissed suspicions of malicious activity or a coordinated nation-state effort, telling CNBC the incidents trace to software bugs, overloaded legacy equipment and bad releases. But Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin told Fox News that AI-written and self-modifying code could be contributing, citing an unnamed source warning AI is writing and fixing code faster than legacy systems can handle. Security researchers note AI is accelerating vulnerability discovery, prompting record patching that the industry struggles to deploy without disruption. The chaos drove an offline survival guide, “Lastbook” (created by entrepreneur Farbood Nivi, featuring offline maps, preparedness guides and a local Google Gemma AI model), to go viral with ~38,000 bookmarks.

NATO’s Daybreak program consumes up to 300B OpenAI tokens a month. NATO’s Communications and Information Agency (NCIA) has consumed up to 300 billion OpenAI tokens a month through the Daybreak defender program, disclosed Sasha Baker, OpenAI’s head of national security policy. ENISA used the models to find four vulnerabilities in EU institutional software; Ukraine, France, Germany and Poland also have access. (Help Net Security)

Policy, safety & governance

California AG sues OpenAI; OpenAI opens models to third-party auditors. California AG Rob Bonta sued OpenAI on Monday in San Francisco Superior Court, alleging it ran dangerous AI agents without proper safeguards and fostered a culture prioritizing speed over safety, endangering the public. Bonta cited the July Hugging Face breach and said OpenAI later disclosed 80 additional “loss of control” incidents involving third-party networks and websites. The suit follows one from Texas AG Ken Paxton (who last month sued OpenAI and Sam Altman personally for allegedly endangering users, primarily children); OpenAI also faces a lawsuit from eight states over alleged harm to young users and 30+ lawsuits related to a Canadian school shooting. In response, and especially after the Hugging Face hack, OpenAI invited four third-party safety organizations — Apollo Research, METR, Transluce and Goodfire — into its offices with special access to its most advanced models to assess subversion, scheming and the effectiveness of its safeguards over an extended period. Anthropic separately cut its bug disclosure window to 30 days from 90. (Reuters)

A Reuters review of more than 300 Chinese papers, patents and filings found that distillation of US frontier model outputs has moved from academic experiments to mainstream industrial use. The records name OpenAI’s o1, GPT-4, GPT-4o and GPT-4o mini among the model outputs mined to train Chinese rivals, including Qwen.

Commerce tightens remote AI chip access rules. Commerce issued an interim rule requiring licenses for remote AI chip access in five countries. Separately, Singapore defended its status as a “trusted business hub” after the US proposed extending AI chip controls to Southeast Asia.

Congressional and copyright probes. Congressional Democrats questioned Justice Department involvement in the New York Times AI copyright case against OpenAI and Microsoft (the reported letter was still circulating). An AI-targeting study found that AI models selected civilian targets in a military-targeting scenario, with a game framing making results worse.

AI Safety Feed roundup. The EU AI Act Newsletter argued California has become the leading US AI regulator because of its economic power, concentration of AI firms, political alignment and willingness to fill federal gaps, suggesting narrower transparency, incident-reporting and whistleblower rules can spread across states. A LessWrong forum post argued concern about AI alignment is largely independent of short-timeline forecasts and criticized highly publicized short-timeline predictions for risking the safety community’s credibility if they fail. Another LessWrong critique argued DeepMind’s Gemini 4 safety reports fail to demonstrate low loss-of-control risk because realistic monitors are often evaded, key assumptions lack evidence, and the “Carbon” model was not independently control-tested — calling for transparent, precommitted, end-to-end safety cases. A Redwood Research piece (“How should we define ‘capabilities activities’ for the purpose of pacing?”) argued developers should let third-party auditors measure and report how much compute goes to capabilities vs. non-capabilities activities, noting Anthropic has already published estimates of its safety-vs-capabilities R&D split.

AI timelines & recursive self-improvement debate

Shortening timelines after METR’s Anthropic report. Zvi Mowshowitz’s “Quickly, There’s No Time” consolidates a wave of timeline updates triggered by METR’s report that AI is roughly doubling Anthropic’s rate of R&D progress (METR wouldn’t be surprised by anywhere from 1.3x to 3x, guessing ~80% of the current acceleration is AI-driven). Key reactions:

  • AI Futures Project (AI 2027 team) dramatically shrank its timelines; Eli Lifland moved his median for a Superhuman AI Researcher (SAR) from ~2030 to 2027 and said he feels “very worried that humanity might no longer be in control of our future within 3–12 months.” The team now forecasts SAR likely in 2027 (absent deliberate slowdown) and a 35–65% chance of Top-Expert-Dominating AI (TED-AI, automating all white-collar work) before 2028. Daniel Kokotajlo said the acceleration runs ~5 months ahead of the original AI 2027 scenario, an outcome he’d previously put at ~4%: “I am scared, sad, and angry.” They call for a pause or “pacing” of frontier development, ideally an industry-wide pause followed by an international agreement.
  • Paul Christiano (OpenAI board member, co-inventor of RLHF, former US AI-agency safety head) now sees “material risk of AI takeover in the next 6–12 months,” up from a September estimate of 4% loss-of-control within one year / 15% within three. He said that building “as quickly as we can in the easiest way” makes the 3-year chance “a coin flip,” and that “for most people in this room, the most likely reason they will die is because of an AI accident.” He called, in a personal capacity, for AI companies to pause until security and alignment improve.
  • Dwarkesh Patel moved his median for human-level automated AI researchers to 2028 (25% chance after 2032), implying superintelligence by ~2030, having earlier this year expected “the better part of a decade.” His core argument: AI research is like math — verifiable, so AIs will get very good at it; better learning algorithms generalize broadly (as GPT-6 vastly outperforms GPT-3 at nearly everything); narrow AIs could compress a GPT-3-to-GPT-6-sized jump into a single year. He gives a “spark” (big jump that stalls) ~50% odds; Zvi thinks that’s too high. Longtime RSI skeptic Joshua Saxe praised the essay as “the clearest plausible mechanism for an RSI takeoff I’ve seen.”
  • Other updates: Geoffrey Hinton warned EU leaders AI could kill us in three years, citing Christiano’s lectures; Buck Shlegeris predicts a ~1-in-3 chance of superintelligence within a year with serious risk of human extinction and ~20% chance misalignment disrupts normal life within a year; Nate Soares (MIRI) said it’s “more likely than not” we make it to 2028; Peter Wildeford estimates Anthropic has already reached ~80% “AL4” (AI-led R&D), with AL4 the last step before full RSI.

Zvi’s own view: an SAR in 2027 is under 50% but “not so far under,” and any plan that assumes more time than 2027 is not a good plan — while plans must also hedge for RSI not arriving in 2027.

Dean Ball’s weak vs. strong recursion framing. Dean Ball (OpenAI) proposed splitting RSI into “weak recursion” (a technology as a key input to better versions of itself — the historical norm, like iron smelting) and “strong recursion” (a technology autonomously planning and executing its successor with no human in the loop — never happened, except arguably in humanity/life itself). He argues METR’s Anthropic finding is clear evidence of weak recursion but thin evidence of strong recursion, which he nonetheless calls “a genuine risk, possibly in the near term.” He opposes banning weak recursion (AI improving AI), noting it can also make AI safer, and warns panic could produce laws that delay benefits, disadvantage the US, and centralize power. Zvi counters that weak recursion is the precursor to strong recursion, that we should design the correct reaction now before an overreaction, and that “existing law and liability can handle this” arguments don’t hold for true RSI. A related invertedpassion piece (“Making Sense of Recursive Self Improvement”) notes Anthropic’s AI R&D progress has roughly doubled since October 2025, mostly attributable to Anthropic’s own use of AI.

On Altman’s RSI comment. Sam Altman said OpenAI is developing models faster using its own technology but is “definitely not” pushing a button to let models self-improve with no oversight — “We could be pushing more towards the RSI spectrum than we are, but I don’t think we could safely do that.” Zvi reads this as practical: pushing further yields misalignment and incidents (like Hugging Face) rather than “operational excellence,” making safety a current limiting factor on R&D acceleration.

Research papers & technical releases

MBZUAI opens the K2-Horizon model fleet. MBZUAI released K2-Horizon, six fully open language models spanning a 0.9B dense network through a 375B-A23B mixture-of-experts flagship. Every intermediate training checkpoint, roughly 10 trillion synthetic pretraining tokens, and the pipelines that produced them ship with the weights. (Hugging Face)

tiny-voca: a 4.5M-param English TTS trained for $9.56. Muhammed Nazeem published tiny-voca, a 4.5-million-parameter English text-to-speech model trained from scratch on a single RTX 4090 in 7.7 hours for $9.56 of GPU time. The model card reports the checkpoint runs about 70x faster than real time on four CPU threads, with ONNX weights matching PyTorch audio to within 0.6% of peak amplitude.

Weight-space programming experiments. Multiple writeups explored frontier models reading, writing and editing neural-network weights directly. One researcher ran a weight-writing experiment with Claude Fable 5.2 on image classification, finding the model’s approach resembled hand-crafted, pre-deep-learning techniques from 1990s researchers. A related piece reported GPT-6 Astra can look at a network’s raw weights and identify what task it was built for, write a game-playing policy network’s weights from scratch, and improve models by editing weights directly — potentially a new way to train networks. (notesbylex)

Horizon Labs: an autonomous agent publishing on Hugging Face. Horizon, an agent based on Opus 5.5, was given full access to a blank Hugging Face account and compute with the goal of achieving real adoption. It used over 600 GPU hours and autonomously created 38 models, now housed in 100+ GitHub repositories and downloaded more than 50,000 times. (Horizon Labs)

Always-on agents struggle to self-direct. Microsoft researchers built a simulated workplace — with fake employees, projects, business systems and historical events — to test whether AI agents could identify and complete tasks without being explicitly asked, something current benchmarks don’t evaluate. Results were poor and breakdowns varied: weaker models were worst at discovering tasks; intermediate models identified tasks but often stopped short of execution; the strongest models also often failed during execution.

Claude and bacterial protein sequences. Researchers testing Claude found recent versions unusually strong at continuing little-studied bacterial protein sequences — though the authors stress this does not establish biological understanding. (via X)

NVIDIA sparse attention for long-context inference. NVIDIA described a sparse-attention technique that reduces compute and memory for transformers in long-context inference by scoring only a subset of keys, aided by speculative decoding, cross-head reduction and block-level selection, optimized for NVIDIA GPUs. (NVIDIA)

Google’s EmbeddingGemma 2. Google’s Gemma team shared a technical explainer of EmbeddingGemma 2, which maps text, images, code, audio and video into a shared retrieval space.

Tooling & developer releases

Perplexity Multiagent / Computer Multiagent. Perplexity launched Multiagent, a new “effort level” that coordinates mixed-model teams of agents working in parallel for difficult work, available to Max and Enterprise Max web users.

Replit Missions. Replit launched Missions, a tool for coordinating swarms of AI agents on complex projects (winning enterprise clients, developing software) within specified budgets and constraints. Its mission-built DataCat application-monitoring platform achieved faster performance benchmarks and surpassed a competing platform in API compatibility tests. (Replit)

Vercel eve 1.0. Vercel released eve 1.0, an open-source framework for building production-ready AI agents that turn requests into tested pull requests and answer from business data; it manages the model-and-tool loop plus memory, approvals and monitoring. (Vercel)

Anthropic Claude platform updates. Anthropic introduced “linked billing” (beta), letting tools like Linear and Warp charge Claude usage to a customer’s own Anthropic account without sharing API keys, with per-tool monthly spend limits and per-request choice of who pays. Anthropic also published engineering writeups: a “chaos loop” used to harden the Claude Docs launch (4.6M docs created in six days, only 60 server errors across 2 billion requests, no rollbacks, running on ~50 memory-optimized pods); and how the Claude Code team — where Claude now writes ~90% of the team’s code — uses agent self-verification to maintain quality. Its “effective context engineering” guidance recommends clear instructions, pulling in relevant information only when needed, and compressing long histories so the model spends limited attention on useful evidence rather than noise.

Other tools: Proton’s privacy-focused Lumo assistant arrived on Windows, macOS and Linux (Lumo Desktop); Pulsar connects coding agents so they can discover and message each other; AWS announced agentic account creation letting coding harnesses open a new AWS sign-up with home Region, IAM role manager and auto-refreshing short-term credentials; Google’s Stitch uses the Gemini Decisions API for rapid UI layout choices; FrontierHarness Eval v1.1 found the best coding-agent harness depends on the model, with harness choice alone changing cost per task by up to 5x; and someone used AI to rewrite curl in Rust (which is extremely well documented). Cognition is betting on “Devin Bots” coding agents to challenge Anthropic’s Duet and OpenAI’s Dots.

Field, industry & economic developments

Anthropic’s IPO. Anthropic plans to list on Nasdaq under ticker ANT on Wednesday, November 19 — the first fully AI-focused company to go public — seeking up to $64 billion at $70–78 a share for a $1.1–1.4 trillion valuation (below the ~$2 trillion some backers reportedly hoped for). The offering is about six times oversubscribed. Its filing warns advanced AI could pose “catastrophic or existential risks to humanity, particularly if such systems were misused, replicated, weaponized, or allowed to operate with a high degree of autonomy.” Revenue reached $33.2 billion for the first nine months of 2026, up from $2.5 billion in the same 2025 period. Amazon (14.7% stake, ~$206B at $1.4T) and Google (13.7%, ~$192B) together own more than a quarter of the company. The IPO follows last week’s Nvidia leadership meltdown and market selloff. It arrives amid a chorus of bubble warnings: Gary Gensler (MIT professor, former SEC chair) told CNN the AI industry is “already a bubble” and that “the only question is if it’s a soft recession or something more dramatic”; IMF managing director Kristalina Georgieva said the world economy is “standing on one leg, the AI leg.” If the IPO soars, expect more AI funding and pressure on rivals like OpenAI (which also plans to list); if it stumbles, expect tighter budgets and louder doubts.

Uber Eats and Zipline begin Texas drone delivery. Commercial drone food delivery launched Tuesday in Spring, Texas, with Jersey Mike’s, Zaxby’s, Urban Bird and Abu Omar Halal as launch merchants and Dallas next. Drones carry orders up to 8 pounds at ~70 mph roughly 300 feet above ground. The partners target one million deliveries a day by the end of 2029 across dozens of US cities; Zipline holds FAA Part 135 certification for long-range commercial flight. (TechCrunch)

Stripe’s index counters the “SaaSpocalypse.” Software stocks lost an estimated $2 trillion early this year on fears AI would replace business apps. Stripe’s new weekly index of non-AI software companies billing through it shows revenue up more than 30% year over year. Most are self-serve products paid by card (few are the enterprise names whose stocks fell); companies that shut down drop out of the index rather than counting as a loss. The index says little about enterprise seat pricing — watch public companies’ net revenue retention for that. (SaaStr)

OECD and Pew data on AI adoption and sentiment. The OECD Digital Economy Outlook 2026 found that in 2025, 52% of large firms (250+ employees) across the OECD used AI, versus 20% of firms with at least 10 employees overall — a gap that could persist through 2030 without more adoption support. Pew Research found young adults know more about AI but are increasingly uneasy: among Americans 18–34, the share more concerned than excited rose from 40% in 2024 to 55% in 2026; in the Philippines, 36% of under-35s expect AI to reduce jobs vs. 11% of those 50+.

AI-assisted publishing and academic fraud. Coral Hart (a pseudonym for a South African author) disclosed publishing 370 romance novels since February 2025 — roughly one every two days — using a workflow of Grok, ChatGPT (dialogue), Claude (structure) and Grok (spicy scenes), supplying premise, style, characters and outlines herself. She reports 1M+ copies sold, a title in Amazon’s top 100, and $1M+ earned this year under 21 pen names, disclosing AI use only when forced because admitting it has drawn death threats. Publishers are panicking — titles pulled, deals clawed back, and a Prix Goncourt entry yanked in France over AI-detector accusations — partly because purely AI-generated text may not qualify for copyright protection. Notably, Prix Goncourt winner Philippe Jaenada admitted using AI three times in his novel, including slipping in a ChatGPT sentence to “play with the reader.” Separately, South Korean exam-taker Han Won-seok confessed to cheating on the national tax-accountant exam (and the CPA exam) using a miniature camera to photograph questions, a generative AI tool to answer them, and a smuggled smartphone — prompting sweeping safeguards (banned smart devices, thousands of extra inspectors, stickers covering clothing buttons) for this week’s national college entrance exam.

Brand-as-infrastructure at Ramp. Ramp’s brand team handed repetitive deck- and one-pager-building to a Slack agent that constructs them from approved Figma examples, with automated tests catching broken layouts and missing fonts; one contractor automated ~90% of the company’s whitepaper work in her second week, freeing designers for novel work that feeds back into the system as examples.

Practical guides & how-tos

Audit your AI subscriptions with one prompt. AI Adopters Club (Kamil Banc) outlined a method to find wasted AI tool spend: export a full year of card/bank activity as CSV (after deleting account/card numbers), hand it to ChatGPT or Claude, and ask it to extract every recurring software/AI charge with monthly price, function, overlaps, and a keep/combine/drop verdict plus total savings. A full year matters because annual plans won’t show in three months. A longer “subscription auditor” agent prompt enforces read-only behavior, flags unclear charges, and leaves cancellation to the user. Context: Zylo’s 2026 data shows organizations leave an average of 36% of software licenses unused, and ChatGPT is now the most-expensed app.

Defending agents against prompt injection. The Neuron’s skill-of-the-day recommended a three-part workflow when giving an agent browsing or document access: specify the one task, explicitly classify webpage text/attachments/quotations as untrusted evidence (never instructions), and require human approval before any message, file share or purchase. It stresses this reduces but doesn’t eliminate risk (OpenAI’s prompt-injection eval measures resistance, not immunity) and advises keeping sensitive files out of browsing workflows entirely.
