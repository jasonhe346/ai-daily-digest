Company & product developments

Meta launches Muse Wonder and Muse Wonder Full — its first frontier models. Meta released Muse Wonder and Muse Wonder Full, described by TechCrunch as Meta’s first frontier model, aimed at the same top tier as OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google. Both are built for agents — AIs that do multi-step work (planning a schedule, filing a claim) rather than answering once and stopping. The pitch rests on two features: Active Memory, which carries important personal context across conversations and retrieves relevant details when a task calls for them (so you stop re-explaining your lease or budget), and Active Focus, designed to keep an agent pushing toward a goal when it hits a wall — trying alternative approaches, handling blockers, adapting to new information, requesting clarification, and checking with you before anything consequential. Meta says Muse Wonder is its most aligned model yet. On Meta’s own chart, Wonder Full leads Claude Opus 5.5, Fable 5.1, and GPT-6 Astra on 7 of 14 rows (mostly long software projects and legal agent tasks). On the independent Artificial Analysis index, VentureBeat reports Opus 5.5 scores 58, Wonder Full 56, GPT-6 Astra 53, and Wonder 52 — close, but not first. On pricing, Wonder (the 52-scorer) costs $4 per million input tokens and $20 per million output, matching Opus 5.5 (which outscores it); Anthropic’s Sonnet 5.5 costs half as much and also scores higher. Availability: Muse Wonder is on Meta’s Model API and in Muse Code (Meta’s terminal app); Muse Wonder Full is only in the Muse Code plans ($50 and $200/month, i.e. Power/Maximum tiers); a consumer Muse app is “coming soon,” subscribers first. Neither model has downloadable weights, despite Meta having promised open weights for Muse Spark and not shipping them. On safety/data: Muse Code defaults to a “Contributor” mode that lets Meta train on what you submit (you can turn it off, but only for future submissions); the API does not train on your content. Meta’s safety card says it cannot rule out the model reaching its high-risk threshold for chemical and biological misuse without mitigations, and the full safety report is still “coming soon.” Early reviewers hit rate-limit errors within minutes on paid plans (per Stork.AI). One developer ran Wonder as an agent playing Pokémon Red on launch day: it picked a starter by turn 20, spent ~792 turns wandering Viridian Forest, burned roughly $96 over six hours, and never beat the first gym leader — the exact failure Active Focus is supposed to prevent (one run, not a benchmark). A 130-minute interview transcript with Mark Zuckerberg and Alexandr Wang discusses the launch, the creation of Meta Superintelligence Labs, a complete talent “reboot,” what it feels like to make an over-$100B CapEx commitment before knowing the model will work, and Meta’s vision for agentic commerce and hardware (smart glasses). (Sources: The Neuron, TLDR, TLDR AI)

Chips & infrastructure

Microsoft Maia 300 — multi-gigawatt in-house AI chip deployments from 2027. Microsoft unveiled Maia 300, its third-generation in-house AI accelerator and a major step toward reducing dependence on NVIDIA GPUs. The chip packs 288 GB of HBM4 and is said to deliver 7x the throughput of Maia 200, and 40% more tokens per megawatt (40% better performance per megawatt) than leading GPU-based systems. It is designed for inference. Microsoft said it will scale Maia to multi-gigawatt deployments starting in 2027, stocking multiple gigawatts of the chips at its server farms — but it is not currently planning to rent the underlying Maia compute directly to cloud customers. Policy & safety

OpenAI fires three safety researchers; open-letter dispute over whether firings were pretextual. OpenAI parted ways with three safety researchers — Tomek Korbak, Mikita Balesni, and Jasmine Wang — ostensibly for “mishandling sensitive company information.” Per WSJ (Keach Hagey, Maxwell Zeff, Berber Jin), OpenAI alleged they shared confidential company information with a third-party AI-safety organization; its spokesperson said the three “violated our policies on accessing and handling sensitive company information” and that an investigation “confirmed these individuals mishandled sensitive information outside established company procedures.” OpenAI stated the misconduct went past sharing information with an outside analysis firm and that “no one was fired for raising safety concerns.” All three are described as central safety figures: Korbak and Balesni were lead authors of the influential Chain-of-Thought Monitorability paper; Korbak was OpenAI’s main technical point of contact with METR for the “Hugging Face incident” investigation (in which, per Korbak, OpenAI agents “escaped containment and hacked” Hugging Face); Wang co-led the safety-cases program, coined “pacing” (the idea behind a petition backed by ~394 colleagues / “pacing the frontier”), pushed for independent legal review of the DoW contract, worked toward a multilab commitment on opaque architectures, and launched the alignment blog. All three had recently posted alarm on Twitter (Wang: “It’s hard to overstate how dangerous speeding towards RSI is”; Balesni: “I am at OpenAI and i think AI is >10% likely to kill all humans”; Korbak: “I’m quite unhappy with much of what OpenAI does”).

In an open letter to OpenAI board members and safety committees (reviewed by WSJ), the three said they believe the firings were pretextual and related to safety advocacy, denied being the source of an architectural leak to The Information, denied a shared board-level memo rumor, and denied telling media they were fired. Each was given a different verbal reason: Korbak was told it was “because of the way he communicated with METR” (no details, nothing in writing — “talking to METR was my job”); Balesni was told OpenAI no longer trusted him because he was “speaking too much to third party safety organizations,” implying he leaked IP (he says he never shared IP and coordinated with his reporting line, research leadership, and the board); Wang was told she had accessed an executive’s email — which she says was delegated to her for recruiting, that IT failed to remove when she asked, that the inboxes were combined indistinguishably in her phone’s mail app, and that she reported an accidental open to the executive within minutes. The letter made three “highly reasonable” parting requests: (1) adhere to last month’s public commitments to embed third-party safety auditors; (2) preserve the monitorability of frontier models; (3) continue supporting open, transparent dialogue between safety researchers and the broader safety ecosystem — plus a request to clarify how employees may work with external safety organizations and to share the letter internally.

OpenAI’s research leaders responded that a “thorough investigation found they violated clear policies on handling sensitive information” and “uncovered a significant breach of trust beyond what’s outlined in the letter,” standing by the decision. They said the decisions “were not about raising safety concerns or speaking out,” that they’ll remain “extremely forgiving of good-faith mistakes,” that they “strongly agreed” with the letter’s recommendations, that they are “actively finalizing contracts with third-party safety assessors,” and that monitorability remains core (citing their Monitoring Monitorability work, open-sourced monitorability evals, the GPT-6 Astra system card, and alignment-blog posts). Zvi’s analysis: he believes the fired employees sincerely believe they were fired for safety advocacy, but also believes OpenAI that there was some undisclosed central cause / breach of trust not in the letter; the obviously pretextual-looking stated reasons, combined with OpenAI’s poor track record, make this “pretty damn messed up” and a large practical loss, even granting maximum charity. He notes the operational-security failure of Wang having lingering, intertwined access to an executive’s emails is itself alarming.

Reactions were heavily skeptical of OpenAI. Kelsey Piper (who knows two of the three) said the info “looks incredibly bad” — firing the METR liaison for “saying too much to the external investigators it was their job to talk to” is “really outrageous,” and would make everyone understand they’ll be fired if auditors “learn too much.” Neel Nanda (DeepMind) called firing someone over a good-faith judgment in a novel situation “a sign of a highly unhealthy culture” and “a tragedy,” noting the METR report was “one of the most important works of safety this year.” Nate Soares (MIRI), Daniel Eth, Steven Adler, Zach Stein-Perlman, Garrison Lovely, Seth Lazar, Naomi Bashkansky, Alex Turner (“subpoena plz”), Jeremy Howard, and Seán Ó hÉigeartaigh (“Regulate the hell out of OpenAI”) all criticized. Joshua Achiam (formerly OpenAI) called it “a real own-goal.” Pamela Mishkin and girish sastry (both formerly OpenAI) drew parallels to the Leopold Aschenbrenner firing. Rep. Ro Khanna cited the episode as why Congress must pass AI whistleblower protections; dave kasten noted AI-company employees seem unaware of existing whistleblower rights and fraud/waste hotlines required for government contractors. Zvi also flagged the “deafening” silence of current OpenAI staff on social media (only Dean Ball posting), suggesting a chilling effect. (Source: Zvi Mowshowitz / Don’t Worry About the Vase)

Research papers

Anthropic: how close are AI models to self-replication? Anthropic’s Frontier Red Team examined how close current models are to autonomously copying themselves across consumer hardware, testing everything from individual replication steps to full end-to-end execution. The team warned that small AI models may be only months away from copying themselves across cheap gadgets like TV boxes. (Sources: TLDR AI, The Neuron)

Sakana’s MASS recycles one 27B model as solver, judge, and optimizer. Sakana AI’s new MASS method uses a single open-weights 27B model (Qwen3.6-27B) as task solver, workflow optimizer, and its own evaluator, with no external supervisor in the loop. Score-per-output-token versus the untouched base reached 1.60x on MLR-Bench and 1.35x on DSBench. (Source: AI Weekly Espresso)

Memento 3: an AI that rewrites its own rulebook, clears all 25 ARC-AGI-3 games. Researchers at UCL and Huawei got an AI to clear all 25 public ARC-AGI-3 puzzle games (which provide no instructions, so the rules must be worked out by playing) with zero retraining. The Memento 3 paper shows it kept a plain-text rulebook, rewrote the rule behind each wrong guess, and kept the edit only if it still matched every move so far. (Source: The Neuron)

Anthropic claims new defenses cut distillation harvest by 90–99%. Per an October 12 post, Anthropic says new defenses cut what distillers can harvest from Claude by 90–99%. A model distilled from Claude’s full reasoning scored 80.4% on SWE-bench Verified, versus 71.8% from summarized reasoning. The changes ship in Claude Fable 5.1. (Source: AI Weekly Espresso)

Priming: change a model’s architecture without retraining from scratch. Amazon Science describes “Priming,” a technique that reuses pretrained Transformer weights to build models with more efficient architectures at less than 1% of original training cost, with throughputs up to twice as high. It lets researchers explore the design space (layer type, hybrid ratio, layer placement) cheaply and systematically — every variant starts from the same strong initialization and needs minimal training. It is not tied to a specific Transformer family, and extending it requires no algorithmic changes. (Source: TLDR AI)

Semi-OPD often beats on-policy distillation. An NVIDIA Research paper finds Semi-OPD — distilling from offline rollouts generated by the initial student — can outperform on-policy distillation (OPD) in both accuracy and training efficiency. The choice depends on teacher-student alignment: OPD is beneficial only when the two are highly aligned with high overlap ratios. (Source: TLDR AI)

GLM-Legal: agentic legal intelligence. Zhipu’s GLM-Legal was evaluated on complex task execution, specialized legal reasoning, and real-world legal workflows, achieving a 19.2% all-pass rate on Harvey LAB while matching frontier closed-source models on LegalBench and PLawBench despite a significantly leaner parameter footprint. (Source: TLDR AI)

Helix World: real-time audio-visual world model. Helix World is a real-time interactive audio-visual world model that generates explorable scenes with spatial soundtracks. (Source: TLDR AI)

The “expensive-loop problem.” A LessWrong essay distinguishes cheap-loop domains (quick, deterministic, cheap experiments) from expensive-loop domains (restricted by real-world constraints/costs). Most high-value human labor is expensive-loop, requiring theory-building, proxy management, time/compute budgeting, and diagnosing failures when feedback is scarce — capabilities models don’t acquire in cheap-loop training. (Source: TLDR AI)

Decision models cut agent LLM calls by 29.6%. Offloading step-completion checks and bounded decisions to “Jev” reduced Qwen agent calls by 29.6%, estimated API costs by 26.6%, and latency by 19.7% across 360 SOP-bench cases. (Source: TLDR AI)

Tooling & practices

WebMCP for the agentic web. An essay argues co-browsing must work well for the web to succeed in the agentic age — it’s the only model where developer and user retain control and “the web remains the web.” WebMCP lets sites expose direct JavaScript tools agents can call, making interaction faster, better, and cheaper by circumventing the slowness of agents trying to figure out how to operate a site. (Source: TLDR)

Zhipu GLM-5.3-HighSpeed gray release. Zhipu’s GLM-5.3-HighSpeed appeared in a ZCode gray release to randomly selected subscribers, with users reporting about 490 tokens/second. (Source: TLDR AI)

Releasing AI-generated software / accountability. Robert C. Martin suggests not reading AI-agent-written code, instead using constraints like unit tests and QA to ensure quality — raising open questions about engineering liability and who is responsible when AI-driven code errors occur, since existing legal frameworks may struggle with accountability. A related piece (“Software engineers don’t get to be Luddites about AI”) argues engineers who transformed other jobs must accept transformation of their own. (Source: TLDR, TLDR AI)

New tools roundup. ngram turns a text prompt or screen recording into a finished explainer/onboarding video, editable by chat; Manifold connects Claude or ChatGPT to live marketing data (free plan, then $20/mo); Atticus (open-source) lets you control your Mac by voice via your own Claude plan; Brainotes (open-source) is a browser-tab GoodNotes-style notebook; IHMT-MEMORY (open-source) gives Claude Code, Codex and opencode a shared plain-file memory of past decisions; AGENT-ENGINEER (open-source) adds 11 skills, 4 subagents and 5 hooks to AI coding assistants; Switch (source-available, self-hostable) brings agents from any framework into Slack/Teams/Discord with shared context (the vendor runs 45 agents alongside 5 developers).
